# Coherence Practice

> Does this hold together?

You have already explored an important Coherence idea:

Individual pieces can look reasonable while the complete structure does not.

Now we are going to practice finding where that happens.

The goal is not to become suspicious of everything.

The goal is to become comfortable asking:

> What supports this?

> What connects these pieces?

> Is something missing?

> Can these claims all hold together?

> Where does the structure begin to break?

---

# Start With the Whole

Coherence asks us to step back.

Awareness may begin with:

```text
What am I observing?
```

Alignment may ask:

```text
What corresponds?
```

Coherence adds:

```text
Do these observations and relationships
work together as a whole?
```

That last part matters.

A structure may contain several individually reasonable pieces and still have a problem when those pieces interact.

---

# A Simple Coherence Record

When practicing, begin with:

```text
Pieces:
What are the important parts?

Relationships:
How are they connected?

Supported:
What currently fits?

Tension:
What does not fit?

Missing:
What may be absent?

Assumptions:
What must be true for this to work?

Current Judgment:
Does this hold together?
```

You do not need every field for every problem.

Use enough structure to make the reasoning visible.

# Practice 1 — The Morning Plan

Imagine:

```text
Goal:
Leave the house by 8:00.

Plan:
Wake at 7:45.
Shower.
Make breakfast.
Feed the animals.
Find the car keys.
Leave by 8:00.
```

Every individual step is reasonable.

But does the plan hold together?

Maybe.

Maybe not.

Ask:

> Is enough time available?

> Are any steps dependent on another?

> Is there an assumption hiding in the schedule?

> What information would you need before deciding?

Notice the difference between:

> This plan is impossible.

and:

> I do not yet have enough information to know whether the plan fits the available time.

The second may be the more coherent conclusion.

---

# Practice 2 — The Helpful Shortcut

A group agrees:

```text
Important changes should be reviewed by the group.
```

Later:

```text
A mistake is discovered just before the deadline.

One member fixes it without review.

The change improves the result.

Everyone agrees afterward that the fix was useful.
```

Does this hold together?

Do not begin with:

> The person was right.

or:

> The person broke the rules.

Instead ask:

- Was the rule absolute?
- Was there an emergency exception?
- Does fixing an error count as an important change?
- Did the group value the outcome more than the process?
- Did the rule remain the same after the event?
- Is an unstated exception doing the work?

The Coherence problem may not be the action itself.

It may be the relationship between:

```text
Declared Rule
      ↕
Actual Behavior
```

---

# Practice 3 — The Better Study Plan

Imagine a learner says:

```text
I want to understand the subject rather than memorize it.

Understanding takes time.

I will not move forward until I completely understand each section.

If something is unclear, I restart the section.

I am working hard but barely moving through the material.
```

Each statement sounds reasonable.

Together, though, something interesting happens.

Ask:

> Does the strategy support the goal?

> Could later material help explain earlier material?

> Is complete understanding realistic at every stage?

> Is temporary uncertainty being treated as failure?

The strategy designed to support understanding may also be preventing the learner from encountering information that would improve understanding.

Coherence often appears in relationships, not isolated statements.

---

# Practice 4 — Find the Missing Link

Consider:

```text
The server became slow.

A configuration file was changed yesterday.

Therefore the configuration change caused the slowdown.
```

Something may be missing.

The configuration change could be responsible.

But the reasoning currently looks like:

```text
Event A happened.
Event B happened later.
Therefore A caused B.
```

Ask:

> What relationship has actually been established?

> What evidence connects the configuration change to the slowdown?

> What other explanations remain possible?

> What observation would strengthen the proposed connection?

The issue is not that the conclusion must be false.

The issue is that the current structure does not yet fully support it.

---

# Missing Is Different From Wrong

This is an important Coherence habit.

Suppose:

```text
A
 ↓
?
 ↓
C
```

You may not have contradiction.

You may have a missing relationship.

So distinguish:

```text
Contradiction
```

from:

```text
Gap
```

from:

```text
Uncertainty
```

from:

```text
Unsupported conclusion
```

They may require different responses.

---

# Practice 5 — Classify the Problem

For each situation, decide whether you see:

```text
Contradiction

Missing relationship

Unsupported conclusion

Uncertainty

No obvious coherence problem
```

### Situation A

```text
The instructions say the device must remain offline.

The next step requires uploading data to a remote server.
```

### Situation B

```text
The plant stopped growing.

The room temperature recently changed.

The temperature change caused the problem.
```

### Situation C

```text
Two measurements disagree.

You do not yet know which measurement is more reliable.
```

### Situation D

```text
The software must preserve existing files.

The update modifies the program but leaves user files unchanged.
```

Do not worry about matching a hidden answer key.

Explain why you classified each one the way you did.

# Contradiction

A contradiction occurs when important claims cannot all hold under the same interpretation and conditions.

For example:

```text
The door remained locked for the entire hour.

The door was unlocked halfway through the hour.
```

If:

- both statements refer to the same door;
- the same hour;
- the same meaning of locked;

then something does not hold together.

Before calling it contradiction, verify the boundary.

Maybe there were two doors.

Maybe the times differed.

Maybe one statement used another meaning.

Coherence depends on good Awareness and Alignment.

---

# Practice 6 — Check the Boundary First

Consider:

```text
The system is offline.

The system is serving requests.
```

Contradiction?

Maybe.

But first ask:

> Which system?

> Which service?

> Which network?

> Which time?

> What does offline mean here?

A system may be:

```text
offline from the public network
```

while still:

```text
serving requests on a local network
```

What looked contradictory may become coherent after the boundary is clarified.

---

# Same Words, Different Meaning

Alignment already introduced a danger:

Two people may use the same word differently.

That can create an apparent Coherence problem.

For example:

```text
Person A:
The service is available.

Person B:
The service is unavailable.
```

Before deciding that one person is wrong, ask what each means by:

```text
available
```

Perhaps:

```text
A:
The process is running.

B:
Users cannot reach it.
```

Both statements may then fit.

Clarity helps Coherence.

---

# Practice 7 — Define the Important Word

Choose one term from a problem:

```text
working
finished
safe
available
aligned
successful
complete
stable
```

Write:

```text
In this problem, I mean:
...
```

Then reconsider the structure.

Did the apparent contradiction remain?

A surprising number of coherence problems begin as definition problems.

---

# Unsupported Conclusion

Sometimes the pieces do not contradict each other.

The problem is that the conclusion goes farther than they support.

For example:

```text
Three students found the exercise helpful.

Therefore every student will benefit from the exercise.
```

The first statement may be true.

The second does not follow from it.

That is not necessarily contradiction.

It is an unsupported extension.

---

# Practice 8 — How Far Does the Evidence Reach?

Consider:

```text
The new software version loaded faster on my computer.

Therefore the new version is faster on every computer.
```

Ask:

> What was actually observed?

> What conclusion is being made?

> What additional evidence would be needed?

Then revise the claim so it matches the observation more closely.

For example:

```text
The new version loaded faster in this test on this computer.
```

That may be less dramatic.

It is also more inspectable.

---

# Coherence and Assumptions

Many explanations depend on assumptions.

That is not automatically a problem.

The problem begins when the assumption disappears from view.

Consider:

```text
The battery is charged.

The device is connected correctly.

Therefore the device should start.
```

Maybe.

But perhaps the reasoning also assumes:

```text
The device itself is functional.
```

Now the structure becomes:

```text
Battery charged
+
Connected correctly
+
Device functional
      ↓
Should start
```

The hidden assumption mattered.

---

# Practice 9 — Expose the Hidden Assumption

Consider:

```text
The password is correct.

Therefore login should succeed.
```

What else may need to be true?

Possible considerations might include:

- the account exists;
- the account is permitted to log in;
- the authentication service is available;
- another required authentication step has been completed.

Do not try to list every possible condition in the universe.

Find the important assumptions for the problem.

---

# Too Many Assumptions Can Be a Clue

Suppose an explanation requires:

```text
Assumption 1
Assumption 2
Assumption 3
Assumption 4
Assumption 5
Assumption 6
```

to preserve one conclusion.

That does not automatically make the explanation wrong.

But it should make you curious.

Ask:

> Is there a simpler explanation?

> Are these assumptions independently supported?

> Did we add them only after the original explanation began failing?

Sometimes adding assumptions repairs a model.

Sometimes it protects a model from evidence that should have changed it.

---

# Practice 10 — Repair or Protect?

Imagine:

```text
Prediction:
The system should remain stable.

Observation:
The system became unstable.
```

Then someone says:

```text
It only became unstable because conditions were unusual.
```

Ask:

> Were those unusual conditions part of the original model?

> Were they measured?

> Does the explanation predict when instability should occur?

> Is the model being improved?

> Or is an exception being added only because the prediction failed?

You do not need to answer immediately.

The important skill is recognizing the distinction.

---

# Local Coherence vs. Global Coherence

A piece can make sense locally while contributing to a larger problem.

Imagine software where:

```text
Module A works.

Module B works.

Module C works.
```

But:

```text
A sends Celsius.

B expects Fahrenheit.

C assumes the value is unitless.
```

Each module may work according to its own local rules.

The complete system may not.

That is a global Coherence problem.

---

# Practice 11 — The Three Modules

Consider:

```text
Sensor:
reports temperature as 20

Processor:
receives 20

Display:
shows 20
```

Looks coherent.

Now add:

```text
Sensor unit:
Celsius

Processor interpretation:
Fahrenheit

Display label:
°C
```

The values align numerically.

The meanings do not.

Ask:

> Where does the system stop holding together?

This is why Alignment of meaning matters before Coherence of the whole.

---

# Coherent Does Not Mean Correct

This distinction matters.

A fictional story can be internally coherent.

A mathematical model can be internally coherent.

A mistaken explanation can even be internally coherent if its assumptions are wrong.

Coherence asks:

> Do these pieces fit together under the supplied structure?

It does not automatically ask:

> Is every premise true?

Evidence still matters.

---

# Practice 12 — Coherent but Unsupported

Imagine:

```text
Assumption:
Every red object is magnetic.

Observation:
This object is red.

Conclusion:
This object is magnetic.
```

The reasoning can be coherent given the assumption.

But ask:

> Is the assumption supported?

This separates:

```text
Internal Coherence
```

from:

```text
Empirical Support
```

That distinction will matter again in the Lean exercises.

---

# Evidence Can Be Messy but Real

The opposite can happen too.

Suppose several observations seem inconsistent.

That does not always mean reality is incoherent.

It may mean:

- measurements differ;
- observers used different boundaries;
- data is missing;
- terminology differs;
- the system changed between observations.

Coherence is something we investigate.

Not something we impose.

---

# Don't Force Coherence

This is one of the most important practice rules.

If two observations do not fit yet, avoid inventing a relationship simply because you want the story to make sense.

It is acceptable to record:

```text
Observation A:
supported

Observation B:
supported

Relationship between A and B:
uncertain
```

That may be the most honest structure available.

---

# Practice 13 — Leave the Gap Open

Consider:

```text
The network slowed down.

A backup job was running.

The slowdown ended later.
```

It is tempting to write:

```text
Backup job
   ↓
Network slowdown
```

Instead try:

```text
Backup job:
observed

Network slowdown:
observed

Relationship:
possible, not established
```

Then ask:

> What evidence would help establish or reject the connection?

An open gap can be better than a false bridge.

---

# Coherence Repair

Once you understand where the problem lies, repair may become appropriate.

Possible repairs include:

- correct a definition;
- add missing information;
- remove an unsupported claim;
- expose an assumption;
- change a boundary;
- revise a mapping;
- preserve uncertainty;
- gather another observation.

Notice that repair does not always mean:

> Make the original conclusion survive.

Sometimes repair means changing the conclusion.

---

# Practice 14 — Repair the Structure

Start with:

```text
The software failed after the update.

Therefore the update contains a defect.
```

Possible repair:

```text
The software failed after the update.

The timing makes the update one possible cause.

Additional evidence is needed before identifying the cause.
```

What changed?

The observation remained.

The unsupported certainty was removed.

The revised structure may now be more coherent with the available evidence.

---

# When Not to Repair

Sometimes a gap should remain open.

Suppose:

```text
A and B do not currently fit.
```

You may not yet have enough evidence to decide whether:

- A is wrong;
- B is wrong;
- the mapping is wrong;
- the boundary is wrong;
- information is missing;
- or the system changed.

Do not repair the unknown by guessing.

Preserving the unresolved state is legitimate.

---

# Coherence in Conversation 💬

Conversation gives us everyday coherence problems.

Someone says:

> I want honest feedback.

Then later:

> Why didn't you just agree with me?

Do these statements hold together?

Maybe.

Perhaps:

```text
honest feedback
```

meant:

```text
supportive feedback
```

Perhaps the person's feelings changed.

Perhaps the statements really are in tension.

The first task is not accusation.

It is understanding the structure.

---

# Practice 15 — Two Statements

Consider:

```text
Statement 1:
I don't care which restaurant we choose.

Statement 2:
Not that one.
```

Contradiction?

Maybe not.

The first statement may have meant:

```text
I don't care among acceptable options.
```

A missing condition becomes visible.

Everyday Coherence often depends on unstated boundaries.

---

# Coherence in Instructions

Instructions can contain subtle conflicts.

For example:

```text
Keep the answer under 100 words.

Include every technical detail.

Explain every assumption.

Provide three examples.

Give full historical context.
```

Each instruction is understandable.

Together, they may be difficult or impossible to satisfy.

That is a constraint Coherence problem.

---

# Practice 16 — Constraint Check

Write down three requirements from a real task.

Then ask:

```text
Can all three be satisfied at once?

Does one make another impossible?

Is one more important?

Is a priority missing?

Could the boundary change?
```

Sometimes coherence requires deciding how constraints interact.

---

# Coherence in Software 💻

A software system may contain:

```text
Requirement

Implementation

Test

Observed Behavior
```

Ideally:

```text
Requirement
     ↕
Implementation
     ↕
Test
     ↕
Behavior
```

But mismatches happen.

A test may pass while verifying the wrong behavior.

An implementation may follow an outdated requirement.

Observed behavior may expose a missing case.

Coherence asks whether these pieces still describe the same intended system.

---

# Practice 17 — Passing Test, Wrong Goal

Imagine:

```text
Requirement:
Reject invalid passwords.

Test:
Checks that an empty password is rejected.

Result:
Test passes.
```

Can we conclude:

> Invalid passwords are correctly rejected.

Not yet.

The test covers one case.

The requirement is broader.

The structure is incomplete.

A green test is useful evidence.

It is not automatically the entire requirement.

---

# Failed Tests Can Help

A failed test can expose:

- broken behavior;
- incorrect assumptions;
- incomplete requirements;
- outdated expectations;
- or even a bad test.

Do not automatically change code until the test turns green.

Ask:

> Which part is wrong?

That is Coherence applied to software.

---

# Practice 18 — Code or Test?

Imagine:

```text
Expected:
unknown sensor value
    ↓
INSUFFICIENT_DATA

Actual:
unknown sensor value
    ↓
UNCERTAIN
```

The test fails.

Possible reasons:

```text
Code is wrong.

Test is wrong.

State definitions are unclear.

Model is incomplete.
```

Before changing anything, ask:

> What should unknown actually mean in this system?

Coherence begins with the model.

Lean provides an unusually strict environment for asking:

> Does this conclusion actually follow?

# But Lean Checks the Model You Give It

Suppose the model declares:

```text
A
B
```

and Lean proves:

```text
C
```

under those assumptions.

That establishes a formal relationship.

It does not independently establish:

```text
A is empirically true.

B is empirically true.
```

So even formal Coherence has a boundary.

---

# Practice 19 — Four Questions for a Formal Proof

For any Lean theorem, ask:

```text
What was declared?

What was assumed?

What was proved?

What was not proved?
```

If you can answer those four questions clearly, the proof becomes much easier to interpret responsibly.

# AI and Coherence 🤖

AI can be a useful Coherence partner.

Try:

> Find the weakest connection in my explanation.

Or:

> Which conclusion is least supported?

Or:

> Identify an assumption required for this argument to hold together.

Or:

> Don't fix this yet. Show me where the structure becomes difficult to support.

Or:

> Give me an alternative explanation that also fits the observations.

Then inspect the response.

AI can suggest a connection.

That does not make the connection true.

---

# AI Can Be Coherent and Wrong

An AI may produce a beautifully organized explanation based on a false premise.

The reasoning may be internally coherent.

The starting assumption may still be wrong.

So ask:

> Does this hold together?

and separately:

> What supports the premises?

Those are different questions.

---

> How is the answer constructed?

Then evaluate.

# A Fresh Practice Problem

Imagine a small community garden with these rules:

```text
Rule 1:
Anyone may use the shared tools.

Rule 2:
Tools must remain available for all members.

Rule 3:
Members may take tools home overnight.

Observation:
Several tools are regularly unavailable during the day.
```

Ask:

> Do the rules hold together?

Perhaps they do under additional conditions.

Perhaps an unstated return-time rule is missing.

Perhaps Rule 3 conflicts with Rule 2 in practice.

Perhaps availability means something different than assumed.

Your task is not to complain about the garden.

Your task is to expose the structure.

# Coherence Self-Check

Before moving to Mastery, ask:

```text
Can I identify the important pieces?

Can I see the relationships among them?

Can I distinguish contradiction from missing information?

Can I identify unsupported conclusions?

Can I expose important assumptions?

Can I leave uncertainty unresolved when necessary?

Can I locate where a structure stops holding together?

Can I revise the structure without merely protecting my first conclusion?
```

If most of those answers are yes, you are practicing Coherence.

---

# Before Mastery

One last reminder:

Coherence is not:

```text
Everything agrees with me.
```

It is not:

```text
Everything has been made neat.
```

It is not:

```text
I found a story that explains everything.
```

Coherence is closer to:

> The relationships I am claiming fit the available structure, and I can show where uncertainty or tension remains.

That's enough.
