# AI Companion

> Use AI to help you think, not to replace the thinking.

AI assistance is optional throughout 5Guides.

You can complete every Guide without it.

If you choose to use an AI assistant, think of it as a reasoning partner sitting beside you. It can offer another viewpoint, ask questions, provide examples, challenge assumptions, or help you get unstuck.

You remain responsible for deciding what makes sense.

---

# The Basic Relationship

The intended relationship is:

```text
Learner
   ↕
AI Companion
   ↓
Explore
   ↓
Inspect
   ↓
Learner Judgment
```

Not:

```text
Problem
   ↓
AI
   ↓
Answer
   ↓
Done
```

The second path might produce an answer.

The first is much more likely to produce learning.

---

# You Don't Need AI

You do not need AI to:

- begin a Guide;
- complete an exercise;
- take an Awareness Check;
- practice a skill;
- attempt mastery;
- earn a badge;
- reflect on what you learned;
- continue to the next Guide.

AI is an optional companion.

It is not a gatekeeper.

---

# What AI Can Help With

## Another Explanation

Try:

> Explain this another way.

Sometimes a different explanation or analogy is enough to make an idea click.

## Another Example

Try:

> Give me another example like this one, but use bicycles instead of software.

Changing the setting can help reveal whether you understand the underlying pattern rather than only the original example.

## A Challenge

Try:

> Challenge my interpretation.

Instead of asking AI to confirm what you already think, ask it to look for weaknesses.

## An Alternative

Try:

> Give me another plausible interpretation without telling me which one is correct.

Now you have something to compare.

## A Missing Piece

Try:

> What important assumption might I have overlooked?

The suggestion may or may not be right.

Your job is to inspect it.

## A Practice Partner

Try:

> Give me a new Alignment problem, but don't solve it for me.

Now AI becomes a source of practice rather than an answer key.

---

# A Useful Rule

Before asking:

> What is the answer?

try asking:

> What should I be looking at?

That small change keeps more of the reasoning with you.

---

# Ask AI to Question You

AI doesn't always have to be the one answering questions.

Try:

> Ask me one question at a time to help me separate what I observed from what I assumed.

Or:

> Ask me questions that test whether my mapping is justified. Don't give me your conclusion yet.

Or:

> Help me find where this explanation stops holding together by asking questions rather than rewriting it for me.

This turns the conversation into guided inspection.

---

# Awareness With AI 👀

Awareness asks:

> What am I observing?

Useful prompts include:

> Help me separate my observations from my interpretations.

> What assumptions might I be making?

> Give me another possible interpretation.

> What information is missing?

> What should I verify before reaching a conclusion?

> Challenge my interpretation without replacing it with yours.

The goal is not to ask AI to decide reality for you.

The goal is to make your own observation process easier to inspect.

---

# Alignment With AI 🔗

Alignment asks:

> What corresponds?

Useful prompts include:

> Challenge my mapping.

> Which correspondence depends on the strongest assumption?

> Find a mismatch I may have overlooked.

> Suggest a different mapping without assuming yours is correct.

> What evidence would help distinguish between these mappings?

> Which things only look similar but may not actually correspond?

AI can produce candidate mappings quickly.

That makes it useful.

It also makes inspection important.

---

# Ask for a Counter-Mapping

One useful Alignment exercise is:

> Construct the strongest reasonable alternative mapping you can.

Then compare:

```text
My Mapping
     ↕
AI Counter-Mapping
```

Ask:

- What does each preserve?
- What does each ignore?
- Which assumptions differ?
- Which boundary does each use?
- What evidence would help distinguish them?

Disagreement becomes learning material.

---

# Coherence With AI 🧩

Coherence asks:

> Does this hold together?

Useful prompts include:

> Find a contradiction in my explanation if one exists.

> Which conclusion is least supported by what I provided?

> What assumption does this argument need in order to hold together?

> Can all of these statements be true at the same time?

> Don't fix the explanation yet. Show me where it becomes difficult to support.

That last instruction can be especially useful.

AI may naturally try to repair an explanation immediately.

Sometimes you want to locate the problem first.

---

# Don't Let AI Repair Too Quickly

Suppose something does not hold together.

AI may offer a corrected version.

Before accepting it, ask:

> What exactly did you change?

Then:

> Why was that change necessary?

Then:

> Did the correction preserve the original meaning, or replace it?

The repair is less important than understanding why the repair was needed.

---

# Clarity With AI 🔎

Clarity asks:

> Can this be understood well enough to inspect?

AI can be useful for trying alternative explanations.

Try:

> Rewrite this more clearly without adding new claims.

> What part of this explanation is ambiguous?

> Which term needs defining?

> What step have I left unstated?

> Give me a shorter version, then tell me what information was removed.

> Compare my original and revised versions for changes in meaning.

That final check matters.

AI can make something beautifully readable while quietly changing what it says.

Clearer is not automatically more accurate.

---

# Continuity With AI 🔄

Continuity asks:

> What persists through change?

Give AI two states or two versions and ask:

> What changed?

> What remained?

> Which relationship persisted?

> Where did the meaning drift?

> Did any important assumption disappear?

> Which change caused the earlier pattern to break?

Then inspect the answer yourself.

AI may notice changes you missed.

You may notice changes it missed.

That's useful.

---

# AI Output Is an Observation Too

When AI produces an answer, don't automatically treat it as:

> The answer.

Treat it first as:

> Something that has now been presented to me.

Then apply the same toolkit.

## Awareness

What did the AI actually claim?

## Alignment

Does the answer correspond to the evidence or source material?

## Coherence

Does its explanation hold together?

## Clarity

Can I understand what is being claimed and what supports it?

## Continuity

Did important information survive from my question into its answer?

AACCC works on AI output too.

---

# Confidence Is Not Evidence

An AI response may sound:

- polished;
- confident;
- detailed;
- technical;
- certain;
- impressively specific.

None of those qualities automatically establish that it is correct.

A confident sentence is still something to inspect.

Ask:

> What supports this?

> Can I verify it?

> What assumption is being made?

> Would another interpretation also fit?

> What information would change the conclusion?

---

# Specificity Is Not Evidence Either

AI can sometimes produce very specific details.

Specificity can make an answer feel trustworthy.

But a precise unsupported claim is still unsupported.

If an answer contains important:

- names;
- dates;
- measurements;
- quotations;
- equations;
- technical specifications;
- citations;
- historical claims;

verify them using an appropriate source when they matter to your conclusion.

The Five Guides do not turn AI confidence into provenance.

---

# Ask for Provenance

When factual material matters, try:

> What source supports that claim?

> Which part comes from the source and which part is your interpretation?

> Can this claim be independently checked?

> What evidence would I need to verify it?

Then inspect the source itself when practical.

A citation can help.

A citation is not magic.

You still need to determine whether the source supports the claim being made.

---

# Ask AI to Separate Layers

A useful prompt is:

> Separate your answer into observation, interpretation, assumptions, and uncertainty.

Or:

> Label which parts are directly supported and which parts are inferred.

Or:

> Tell me what you know, what you're assuming, and what you would need to verify.

The labels themselves are not automatically correct.

They give you a structure to inspect.

---

# Use AI to Generate Alternatives

AI can be helpful when your thinking feels stuck on one explanation.

Try:

> Give me three different plausible interpretations of this situation.

Then don't immediately ask:

> Which one is right?

Start with:

> What evidence would distinguish among them?

That changes AI from an answer generator into a possibility generator.

You remain responsible for evaluating the possibilities.

---

# Use AI to Argue Against You

Try:

> Assume my conclusion is wrong. What is the strongest reasonable challenge to it?

Or:

> What evidence would weaken my explanation?

Or:

> What would have to be true for my mapping to fail?

People naturally become attached to explanations they've already built.

AI can provide useful friction.

---

# Opposition Is Not Truth

If AI gives you a counterargument, that does not automatically make the counterargument better.

The purpose is not:

> AI disagreed, therefore I'm wrong.

The purpose is:

> Now I have another structure to inspect.

Compare evidence.

Compare assumptions.

Compare boundaries.

Then decide.

---

# Use AI to Change Scale

Sometimes a concept becomes clearer when viewed from another level.

Try:

> Explain this using an everyday example.

Then:

> Explain the same structure using a software system.

Then ask:

> What stayed the same between the two examples?

This is a useful way to practice abstraction.

You're looking for the structural relationship that survives the change in context.

---

# Use AI to Change Perspective

Awareness showed us that observers can have different access to information.

AI can help you experiment with perspective.

For example:

> Describe how this might look from the user's perspective.

Then:

> Describe it from the system administrator's perspective.

Then ask:

> What information does each perspective have that the other may not?

Or:

> Give me two reasonable observer positions that could produce different descriptions of this situation.

Then compare.

---

# Ground First When Using Imagination

Some Awareness exercises use imagination.

The same guideline applies when AI helps guide an imaginative exercise.

> Get comfortable first. Explore second.

Know where you are.

Remember that you can stop.

If an exercise becomes uncomfortable, confusing, or simply unhelpful, pause and return your attention to your surroundings.

AI does not outrank your comfort.

---

# Don't Share What You Don't Need To

You usually do not need to give an AI assistant personal information to practice AACCC.

Avoid unnecessarily sharing:

- full names;
- addresses;
- account credentials;
- private records;
- identifying information about other people;
- private messages;
- confidential documents;
- sensitive personal information.

When possible, replace identifying details with neutral descriptions.

For example, instead of:

> My coworker Jane Smith at Example Corporation said...

you may only need:

> A coworker said...

The structural problem can often be explored without the identity.

---

# External AI Services Have Their Own Rules

5Guides does not control every AI assistant a learner may choose to use.

Different services may have different:

- privacy practices;
- account requirements;
- retention policies;
- capabilities;
- limitations;
- terms.

Before sharing sensitive information, understand the service you're using.

The safest default remains:

> Share only what the learning task actually needs.

---

# AI and Private Reflection

A reflection prompt may ask:

> What changed about how you approached the problem?

You may keep the answer entirely private.

If you choose to discuss it with an AI service, the information is crossing an additional boundary.

That may be perfectly acceptable for the reflection you want to explore.

Just make the choice consciously.

---

# AI and Assessment

AI can help you prepare for a mastery challenge.

It can:

- generate practice examples;
- challenge your reasoning;
- quiz your assumptions;
- explain difficult ideas;
- provide alternative scenarios.

But the mastery milestone should still represent your demonstrated skill.

Avoid:

```text
Mastery Challenge
      ↓
AI solves it
      ↓
Copy answer
      ↓
Badge
```

Prefer:

```text
Practice
   ↕
AI Companion
   ↓
Fresh Challenge
   ↓
You Apply the Skill
   ↓
Mastery
```

---

# Ask for Practice, Not the Answer

Instead of:

> Solve my mastery challenge.

try:

> Give me a similar practice challenge without solving it.

Or:

> Ask me questions that help me inspect my reasoning, but don't provide the final answer.

Or:

> Tell me which skill I should revisit without solving the problem for me.

That keeps AI inside the learning process without letting it replace the process.

---

# AI and Lean

AI can also help with the optional Lean 4 Lab.

Useful requests include:

> Explain this theorem statement in plain language.

> What assumptions does this Lean definition introduce?

> Why might this proof fail?

> Give me a hint without completing the proof.

> What changed between these two theorem statements?

AI-generated Lean code should still be inspected and compiled.

The compiler gets a vote. 😉

---

# Let Lean Check Lean

When working with Lean:

```text
AI Suggestion
     ↓
Read It
     ↓
Inspect Assumptions
     ↓
Compile
     ↓
Inspect Result
```

If Lean rejects the proof, don't automatically ask AI for a more powerful tactic.

First inspect the error.

It may indicate:

- the proof is incomplete;
- an assumption is missing;
- a definition differs from what you expected;
- an expected library is unavailable;
- the theorem itself does not follow.

Compiler failure can be evidence.

---

# AI Can Help Read Compiler Errors

A useful request is:

> Explain this compiler error without changing the theorem yet.

Then ask:

> What are the possible causes?

Then:

> Which cause is most directly supported by the error message?

Now you're using AI as a troubleshooting partner rather than a code slot machine.

---

# Keep the Human in the Loop

For 5Guides, keeping the human in the loop means more than clicking Approve.

It means the learner remains actively responsible for:

- interpreting;
- comparing;
- questioning;
- checking;
- revising;
- deciding.

AI may contribute observations or possibilities.

It should not quietly become the final authority.

---

# Notice When You're Outsourcing the Interesting Part

Sometimes the best part of an exercise is the struggle.

You're staring at something and thinking:

> Hmm... that doesn't quite fit.

Then you notice why.

That moment is valuable.

If AI immediately explains every difficult part, you may receive the conclusion while missing the discovery.

Occasionally ask yourself:

> Would I learn more by sitting with this for another minute before asking?

Sometimes yes.

Sometimes no.

Awareness includes noticing the difference.

# A Simple AI Pause

Before sending a prompt:

```text
What do I currently think?
        ↓
What am I uncertain about?
        ↓
What do I want help inspecting?
        ↓
Ask
```

After receiving the answer:

```text
What did it claim?
        ↓
What changed in my understanding?
        ↓
What should I verify?
        ↓
What do I think now?
```

That small pause keeps the conversation anchored in your own reasoning.

# Good Prompt Patterns

## Awareness

> Ask me questions that help separate observation from interpretation.

## Alignment

> Challenge my mapping and identify one possible mismatch.

## Coherence

> Locate where my explanation becomes unsupported, but don't repair it yet.

## Clarity

> Make this easier to inspect without adding new claims. Then list what you changed.

## Continuity

> Compare these two states and separate what changed from what persisted.

These prompts ask AI to support a process rather than simply provide a conclusion.

---

# A Reusable AI Companion Prompt

When you want AI assistance during any Guide, try:

> Act as my 5Guides thinking partner.
>
> Do not jump directly to the answer.
>
> Help me inspect the problem using Awareness, Alignment, Coherence, Clarity, and Continuity where appropriate.
>
> Ask questions, expose assumptions, preserve uncertainty, and distinguish observation from interpretation.
>
> If you suggest an answer, explain what supports it and what remains uncertain.
>
> Challenge me when useful, but leave the final judgment to me.

You can shorten or adapt this however you like.

The important relationship is:

> Help me inspect.

not:

> Think instead of me.

---

# When AI Says It's Sure

You don't need to argue with it.

Ask:

> What evidence makes you confident?

Then:

> What would make you change that conclusion?

A useful explanation should survive inspection better than confidence alone.

---

# When AI Changes Its Mind

If the answer changes after you provide additional information, ask:

> What new information changed your conclusion?

Then:

> Which part of the earlier answer no longer holds?

That's a Continuity exercise.

You can inspect the transition:

```text
Earlier Answer
      ↓
New Information
      ↓
Revised Answer
```

The difference between versions may reveal which assumptions mattered.

---

# When Two AIs Disagree

Don't immediately ask a third AI to vote. 😉

Instead compare:

- what each claims;
- what evidence each uses;
- what assumptions differ;
- what sources they rely on;
- what remains uncertain.

Then ask:

> What observation would help distinguish between these answers?

That's Alignment and Coherence in action.

---

# When You Disagree With AI

Good.

Now there's something to inspect.

Ask:

> What evidence supports my view?

> What evidence supports the AI's view?

> Are we using the same definitions?

> Are we working inside the same boundary?

> Is one of us assuming something the other isn't?

Disagreement is not a failure of the learning relationship.

It can be the interesting part.

---

# When AI Is Wrong

AI can be wrong.

If you find an error:

1. identify it;
2. explain why it is unsupported or incorrect;
3. provide relevant evidence if available;
4. revise the reasoning;
5. continue.

That's excellent AACCC practice.

You don't need the AI to be infallible for it to be useful.

---

# When You Are Wrong

Also good. 😉

The same process applies.

Notice.

Inspect.

Revise.

Continue.

AACCC isn't designed to protect our first interpretation.

It is designed to help us notice when that interpretation should change.

---

# Don't Turn the Companion Into an Authority

The AI Companion should not become:

> The thing that tells me whether I'm allowed to believe my answer.

It should remain:

> One source of questions, alternatives, explanations, and challenges that I can inspect.

That's a healthier learning relationship.

---

# Use AACCC on the AI Companion

The Companion itself should remain subject to the Five Guides.

## Awareness

> What information did I give the AI, and what did it actually return?

## Alignment

> Does the response correspond to my question and evidence?

## Coherence

> Does its reasoning hold together?

## Clarity

> Can I understand what is being claimed and why?

## Continuity

> Did important meaning survive through the conversation, or did something drift?

If the AI Companion cannot survive those questions, don't exempt it.

Inspect it harder. 😉

# The AI Companion Promise

The role of AI in 5Guides is modest:

- help the learner explore;
- help the learner ask better questions;
- help expose assumptions and alternatives;
- help explain difficult ideas;
- help generate practice;
- help inspect reasoning.

But leave something important with the learner:

> The judgment.

The goal of 5Guides is not to create someone who always knows what to ask an AI.

The goal is to help create someone who can look at a problem and ask:

> What am I observing?

> What corresponds?

> Does this hold together?

> Can I make it clear enough to inspect?

> What persists when it changes?

Then use whatever tools are appropriate without surrendering those questions.
