# Alignment

> What corresponds?

You have reached the second Guide.

Awareness gave you a place to begin:

```text
What am I observing?
```

You practiced separating:

```text
Observation

Interpretation

Assumption

Unknown
```

You noticed boundaries.

You noticed blind spots.

You noticed that two observers can look at the same thing from different positions and honestly report different information.

Good.

Now we can begin connecting things.

The next question is:

> What corresponds?

Welcome to Alignment.

---

# Start With Two Things

Alignment begins when we have something to compare.

Call them:

```text
A

B
```

They might be:

- two objects;
- two observations;
- two descriptions;
- two maps;
- two measurements;
- two versions;
- a model and a system;
- a requirement and an implementation;
- a word and a meaning.

Now ask:

> What relationship exists between A and B?

That is the beginning of Alignment.

---

# A Simple Example

Imagine a key and a lock.

You might observe:

```text
Key shape
```

and:

```text
Lock opening
```

Then propose:

```text
Key
 ↔
Lock
```

Why?

Perhaps the shape corresponds.

Perhaps the key physically fits.

Perhaps you know the key belongs to that lock.

Those are related claims.

They are not identical.

---

# Correspondence Has a Reason

Instead of saying:

```text
A matches B.
```

ask:

> In what way?

For example:

```text
The key corresponds to the lock's shape.

The map marker corresponds to a physical location.

The label corresponds to an object.

The requirement corresponds to a software behavior.
```

The relationship becomes easier to inspect when we say what kind of correspondence we mean.

---

# Alignment Is Not Identity

This distinction matters.

A map may correspond to a landscape.

The map is not the landscape.

A photograph may correspond to a person.

The photograph is not the person.

A variable may correspond to a measurement.

The variable is not the physical measurement.

A model may correspond to some behavior of a system.

The model is not the system.

So:

```text
A ↔ B
```

does not automatically mean:

```text
A = B
```

Alignment is about relationship.

Not identity.

---

# The Map and the Territory 🗺️

Imagine holding a map.

The map contains:

- roads;
- rivers;
- landmarks;
- symbols;
- labels.

The landscape contains:

- physical roads;
- actual water;
- buildings;
- terrain;
- people;
- weather;
- countless details the map does not show.

The map can align very well with the landscape for a particular purpose without containing everything the landscape contains.

---

# Alignment Depends on Purpose

A road map may align beautifully with:

```text
Where should I turn?
```

and poorly with:

```text
What species of tree grows beside this trail?
```

Same map.

Different purpose.

That gives us a useful question:

> What is this representation supposed to correspond to?

---

# Alignment Has a Boundary

Suppose two maps show the same city.

Map A contains:

```text
Roads

Buildings

Parks
```

Map B contains:

```text
Roads

Elevation

Rivers
```

Do they align?

If we compare:

```text
Roads
```

perhaps strongly.

If we compare:

```text
Everything represented
```

only partly.

So before judging Alignment, ask:

> What exactly am I comparing?

---

# The Comparison Boundary

A useful Alignment habit is:

```text
I am comparing:
...

I am not currently comparing:
...
```

For example:

```text
I am comparing:
Road locations and intersections.

I am not currently comparing:
Buildings, elevation, or vegetation.
```

Now another person knows what the Alignment claim includes.

---

# The Charger Game 🔌

You find a cable.

The connector fits your device.

Does that mean:

> Correct charger!

Maybe.

The physical connector aligns.

But other relationships may matter:

```text
Connector shape

Electrical behavior

Power requirements

Supported protocol
```

A relationship can align at one layer and fail at another.

---

# One Match Does Not Prove Every Match

This pattern appears everywhere.

```text
Same connector
```

does not automatically mean:

```text
Same electrical behavior
```

Likewise:

```text
Same word
```

does not automatically mean:

```text
Same meaning
```

And:

```text
Same shape
```

does not automatically mean:

```text
Same function
```

Alignment needs a declared relationship.

---

# Same Word, Different Meaning

Imagine:

```text
Person A:
The project is finished.

Person B:
The project is not finished.
```

At first, that looks like disagreement.

But ask:

> What does finished mean to each person?

Perhaps:

```text
Person A:
finished ↔ software runs

Person B:
finished ↔ software + tests + documentation complete
```

The word aligned.

The meanings did not.

---

# Different Words, Similar Meaning

The opposite can happen too.

One person says:

```text
repair
```

Another says:

```text
fix
```

In one context, those may correspond closely.

In another:

```text
repair
```

might describe restoring something damaged, while:

```text
fix
```

might include configuration changes or replacement.

Words alone do not settle the mapping.

---

# Alignment Needs Evidence

Suppose you propose:

```text
A ↔ B
```

Now ask:

> Why?

A useful Alignment statement includes the relationship and its support.

For example:

```text
Mapping:
Map marker ↔ building entrance

Evidence:
The marker's coordinates correspond to the
entrance location.
```

Now another person can inspect why the mapping was proposed.

---

# Evidence Can Be Weak or Strong

Compare:

```text
These two things both use the number 3.
```

with:

```text
These two diagrams assign the same three functions
to corresponding positions.
```

The first establishes a superficial similarity.

The second may establish a more meaningful structural relationship.

This matters when comparing complex models.

---

# Similarity Is Not Alignment by Itself

Two things can be similar without corresponding in a useful way.

For example:

```text
Triangle
```

and:

```text
Three-person team
```

both involve three.

Does each point of the triangle correspond to one team member?

Maybe, if a model explicitly defines that mapping.

Otherwise:

```text
both have three
```

is only a similarity.

Do not ask a resemblance to do more work than it supports.

---

# Alignment Is Not Provenance

This is especially important.

Suppose two documents contain similar ideas.

That establishes:

```text
similarity
```

It does not automatically establish:

```text
Document A influenced Document B.
```

A historical influence claim requires evidence about:

- chronology;
- access;
- citation;
- documented lineage;
- or another relevant connection.

Structural resemblance is not proof of historical influence.

---

# A Useful Lineage Rule

Keep these separate:

```text
Looks similar to

Corresponds structurally to

Was derived from

Was influenced by

Cites

Revises
```

Those are different relationships.

Good Alignment makes the distinction visible.

---

# Partial Alignment

Many useful relationships are incomplete.

Imagine two bicycles.

Both contain:

```text
Frame

Two wheels

Chain

Pedals

Brakes
```

But:

```text
Bicycle A:
road bike

Bicycle B:
mountain bike
```

At one level:

```text
Strong structural correspondence
```

At another:

```text
Important differences
```

That is partial Alignment.

---

# Partial Does Not Mean Failed

A partial mapping can be exactly what you need.

Suppose you are comparing:

```text
basic drivetrain structure
```

The road bike and mountain bike may align well enough.

If you are comparing:

```text
suspension behavior
```

the Alignment may change dramatically.

Again:

> What is the purpose of the comparison?

---

# Conditional Alignment

A relationship may hold only under certain conditions.

For example:

```text
Documentation
      ↔
Software version 2.1
```

That may be strong Alignment.

After the software changes:

```text
Documentation
      ↔
Software version 3.0
```

perhaps not.

So a more precise statement is:

> This documentation aligns with version 2.1.

The condition belongs to the relationship.

---

# Time Can Change Alignment

Two things may correspond now and drift later.

For example:

```text
Requirement
     ↔
Implementation
```

at release.

Then implementation changes.

If the requirement stays fixed:

```text
Requirement
     ↔
?
```

Alignment is not necessarily permanent.

This will become important when we reach Continuity.

---

# Mismatch Is Useful

Suppose:

```text
Expected:
10

Observed:
12
```

There is a mismatch.

Do not immediately change 12 into 10.

Do not immediately change the expectation either.

First preserve:

```text
Expected:
10

Observed:
12

Difference:
2
```

Now investigate.

---

# The Mismatch Might Mean...

Possibilities include:

- measurement uncertainty;
- model error;
- changed conditions;
- a legitimate tolerance;
- a different system boundary;
- a new phenomenon;
- a bad observation;
- a bad expectation.

The mismatch is information.

Do not erase it before understanding it.

---

# Not Everything Should Align

This is one of the most important Alignment ideas.

Suppose two people observe opposite sides of an object.

Observer A reports:

```text
red label
```

Observer B reports:

```text
no red label
```

Should their observations be made identical?

No.

The difference may be exactly what their positions predict.

Trying to force Agreement would destroy information.

---

# Alignment Is Not Agreement

Alignment asks:

> What relationship connects these observations?

It does not require:

> Make both observers say the same thing.

Sometimes the correct Alignment is:

```text
Observer A
    ↔
Front view

Observer B
    ↔
Back view
```

The mismatch is coherent with the observer positions.

---

# Awareness Still Comes First

Before mapping two observations, ask:

> What did each observer actually observe?

If either record has already mixed observation and interpretation, the Alignment may inherit the confusion.

That is why the Guides build on each other.

---

# Alignment May Send You Backward

Suppose you try to map:

```text
Observation A
      ↔
Observation B
```

and nothing fits.

Before declaring:

```text
Misaligned
```

you may return to Awareness.

Ask:

> Were these even observations of the same thing?

> Were the boundaries the same?

> Were they measured at the same time?

> Did the system change?

Moving backward is allowed.

---

# User and Administrator

Imagine a user reports:

```text
Login is broken.
```

An administrator reports:

```text
Server is healthy.
```

Do they conflict?

Maybe not.

The user observes:

```text
end-to-end login experience
```

The administrator may observe:

```text
server heartbeat or service health
```

Those boundaries differ.

---

# Map the Layers

A better representation might be:

```text
User
 ↓
Login workflow
 ↓
Authentication service
 ↓
Dependency
```

The user may observe failure at the top.

The administrator may observe health in one layer.

Both can be accurate.

Alignment helps determine which observations are actually comparable.

---

# Alignment Across Scale

A map may align with a city at street level.

It may not align with:

```text
every tree

every mailbox

every temporary road closure
```

The representation was not designed for that scale.

Do not demand Alignment outside the scope of the model.

---

# Model Scope

Every model leaves something out.

That is not automatically a defect.

A useful model may intentionally preserve:

```text
Important relationship
```

while omitting:

```text
Irrelevant detail
```

The Alignment question is:

> Does the model preserve what matters for its intended purpose?

---

# Alignment and Loss

Mapping one representation into another may lose information.

For example:

```text
Detailed report
      ↓
One-page summary
```

The summary may preserve:

- major findings;
- key qualifications;
- decisions.

It may lose:

- detailed evidence;
- edge cases;
- methodological nuance.

The relationship can still be useful.

But the information loss should be understood.

---

# The Summary Game

Imagine a 30-page report becomes:

```text
Three bullets
```

Ask:

> What must survive?

Perhaps:

```text
Main conclusion

Critical qualification

Major uncertainty
```

If those disappear, the summary may remain readable while becoming poorly aligned with the source.

This bridges naturally toward Clarity.

---

# Labels and Reality

A system label may say:

```text
HEALTHY
```

The underlying system may have:

```text
partial failure
```

The useful question is:

> What does HEALTHY correspond to?

Perhaps the system defines:

```text
HEALTHY
=
heartbeat responded
```

Then the label may align perfectly with its definition while failing to represent everything a user means by healthy.

Again, Alignment depends on the mapping.

---

# Measurement and Concept

Research often maps:

```text
Concept
    ↔
Measurement
```

For example:

```text
Performance
    ↔
response time
```

But response time may represent only one aspect of performance.

Other aspects might include:

- throughput;
- error rate;
- resource use;
- reliability.

One measurement can align partially with a broader concept.

---

# Operational Alignment

When a concept is represented through a measurement, ask:

```text
What part of the concept is measured?

What part is not?

Why was this measurement chosen?

Under what conditions does the mapping hold?
```

That makes operational definitions easier to inspect.

---

# Alignment in Data 💾

Suppose two datasets contain fields named:

```text
ID

Name

Date

Value
```

Easy match?

Maybe not.

Ask:

```text
Does ID identify the same kind of thing?

Does Name follow the same convention?

Does Date refer to the same event?

Does Value use the same unit?
```

Matching labels can hide semantic mismatch.

---

# Same Schema, Different Meaning

For example:

```text
Dataset A:
Date = order date

Dataset B:
Date = shipment date
```

The field names align.

The meanings do not.

A technical join may succeed while the conceptual join fails.

That is an Alignment problem.

---

# Alignment in Software Requirements

Suppose:

```text
Requirement:
Users can export their data.
```

Implementation:

```text
Users can save a screenshot of the current page.
```

Does that align?

Maybe not.

We need to inspect:

```text
export
```

and:

```text
their data
```

The implementation may provide a related feature without satisfying the intended requirement.

---

# Alignment in Tests

A test may also map to a requirement.

```text
Requirement:
Reject invalid passwords.

Test:
Empty password is rejected.
```

The test aligns with one case.

It does not automatically represent the entire requirement.

A passing test can be real evidence and still be partial.

---

# Alignment and Formal Reasoning

Formal systems make correspondences unusually explicit.

Suppose Lean knows:

```text
a = b
```

That equality justifies certain substitutions.

The lesson is not merely:

> Rewrite `a` as `b`.

The lesson is:

> A declared or proven relationship permits the transformation.

The mapping comes first.

# Alignment and AI 🤖

AI can propose mappings very quickly.

For example:

> Compare these two frameworks.

An AI may produce dozens of correspondences.

Some may be useful.

Some may be superficial.

Some may be unsupported.

The number of mappings does not establish their quality.

---

# Ask AI for Mapping Strength

A useful prompt is:

> Compare these two structures. Label each proposed correspondence as strong, partial, conditional, uncertain, or unsupported. Explain what evidence supports each mapping.

Now the AI has to expose more of the relationship.

You still inspect the result.

---

# Ask AI to Preserve Mismatch

Try:

> Do not force these structures to match. Preserve important differences and unmapped elements.

That instruction can be surprisingly valuable.

Without it, pattern-seeking systems may over-align.

Humans do this too.

---

# Counter-Mapping

Another useful exercise is:

> Give me the strongest reasonable argument that these two things do not correspond.

Now compare:

```text
Mapping
   ↕
Counter-Mapping
```

Ask:

> Which evidence supports each?

Disagreement reveals structure.

---

# Alignment and Provenance

Suppose two ideas resemble each other.

Before claiming:

```text
A influenced B
```

ask for lineage evidence.

Useful evidence might include:

- citation;
- documented access;
- revision history;
- explicit attribution;
- preserved project lineage.

Without that, the strongest responsible statement may simply be:

> A and B share a structural resemblance.

That can still be interesting.

---

# Alignment States

For practice, we will use several descriptive states:

```text
Strong

Partial

Conditional

Uncertain

Misaligned

Unmapped
```

These are not numerical scores.

They help describe the relationship you currently see.

---

# Strong

Use:

```text
Strong
```

when the important relationships inside the chosen boundary correspond well and have useful support.

Strong does not mean identical.

---

# Partial

Use:

```text
Partial
```

when useful correspondences exist but important differences remain.

This is common.

Partial Alignment can be excellent modeling.

---

# Conditional

Use:

```text
Conditional
```

when the mapping depends on a stated condition.

For example:

```text
Strong for software version 2.1.

Weak after version 3.0.
```

The condition belongs to the result.

---

# Uncertain

Use:

```text
Uncertain
```

when the relationship is plausible but not adequately established.

Do not upgrade uncertainty merely because a neat mapping would be convenient.

---

# Misaligned

Use:

```text
Misaligned
```

when important relationships conflict under the chosen comparison.

The mismatch itself may identify what deserves another look.

---

# Unmapped

Use:

```text
Unmapped
```

when no useful correspondence has been established.

This is different from:

```text
Proven impossible to map.
```

Again, preserve the boundary of the claim.

# Mastery Goal

By the end of Alignment, you should substantially be able to:

- identify what is being compared;
- define the comparison boundary;
- propose meaningful correspondences;
- support mappings with evidence;
- identify assumptions;
- preserve important mismatches;
- recognize partial Alignment;

- identify unmapped elements;
- distinguish resemblance from provenance;
- leave relationships uncertain when evidence is insufficient.

The goal is practical mapping.

Not forced agreement.

# Bridge to Coherence

Suppose you've mapped:

```text
A ↔ B

B ↔ C

C ↔ D
```

Each correspondence looks reasonable.

Great.

Now step back.

Can all of them hold together at once?

Does the complete structure make sense?

Did one mapping introduce a contradiction elsewhere?

The next question is:

> Does this hold together?

That's Coherence.
