# Alignment Practice

> What corresponds?

You have explored Alignment through mappings, mismatches, perspective, partial relationships, and the temptation to make things fit when they do not.

Now we are going to practice using Alignment deliberately.

Awareness gave us something important:

```text
Observation A

Observation B

Assumptions

Boundaries

Unknowns
```

Alignment asks what happens when we begin relating those things.

The central question is:

> What corresponds?

And just as importantly:

> What does not?

---

# Start With Two Things

Alignment needs something to compare.

Call them:

```text
A

B
```

They might be:

- two objects;
- two descriptions;
- two measurements;
- two diagrams;
- two versions;
- two systems;
- a model and an observation;
- a word and a definition;
- a requirement and an implementation.

Before mapping anything, identify what A and B actually are.

Awareness still comes first.

---

# The Basic Alignment Record

A useful starting structure is:

```text
Object A:
...

Object B:
...

Boundary:
...

Correspondences:
...

Evidence:
...

Assumptions:
...

Mismatches:
...

Unknowns:
...

Current Judgment:
...
```

You do not need every field for every problem.

Use enough structure to make the proposed relationship inspectable.

---

# Practice 1 — Charger and Port

Imagine you have a charging cable and a device.

The connector appears to fit the device's port.

You might propose:

```text
Connector
    ↔
Port
```

Good start.

But physical fit does not automatically establish:

```text
Compatible charger
```

Other relationships may matter:

```text
Connector shape

Electrical specification

Supported charging protocol

Power requirements
```

Alignment can exist at one layer and fail at another.

---

# Same Shape Is Not Complete Alignment

Consider:

```text
A fits into B.
```

That establishes one relationship.

It does not necessarily establish:

```text
A is designed for B.

A is safe for B.

A performs the required function with B.
```

Alignment should be specific about the relationship being claimed.

---

# Practice 2 — Name the Relationship

Choose two familiar objects.

Instead of writing:

```text
These align.
```

write:

```text
They align with respect to:
...
```

For example:

```text
The key corresponds to the lock's shape.

The map symbol corresponds to a trail.

The filename corresponds to a document.

The plug corresponds physically to the socket.
```

Then ask:

> What kind of Alignment have I actually established?

---

# Alignment Has a Boundary

Imagine comparing two maps.

Map A shows:

```text
Roads

Buildings

Parks
```

Map B shows:

```text
Roads

Elevation

Rivers
```

Do the maps align?

Partly.

If your boundary is:

```text
Road network
```

perhaps strongly.

If your boundary is:

```text
All represented information
```

not completely.

The Alignment judgment depends on what you are comparing.

---

# Practice 3 — Declare the Boundary

Before comparing two things, write:

```text
I am comparing:
...

I am not currently comparing:
...
```

For the maps:

```text
I am comparing:
Road positions and intersections.

I am not currently comparing:
Elevation or building information.
```

Now the Alignment claim becomes clearer.

---

# Correspondence Is Not Identity

If:

```text
A ↔ B
```

that does not necessarily mean:

```text
A = B
```

A map corresponds to a landscape.

The map is not the landscape.

A variable may correspond to a measured quantity.

The variable is not the physical measurement itself.

A model may correspond to some behavior of a system.

The model is not the system.

This distinction matters.

---

# Practice 4 — Corresponds But Is Not

Complete:

```text
A photograph corresponds to:
...

But the photograph is not:
...
```

Then:

```text
A weather forecast corresponds to:
...

But the forecast is not:
...
```

Then create one of your own.

The exercise helps separate representation from represented object.

---

# Names Are Mappings

A name creates a relationship:

```text
Label
  ↔
Object or concept
```

For example:

```text
"rear derailleur"
      ↔
particular bicycle component
```

That mapping is useful.

But labels can drift.

Two people may use the same word for different things.

Or different words for the same thing.

Alignment helps expose that.

---

# Practice 5 — Same Word, Different Meaning

Consider:

```text
Person A:
The project is finished.

Person B:
The project is not finished.
```

Before deciding that one is wrong, map:

```text
Person A:
finished ↔ ?

Person B:
finished ↔ ?
```

Perhaps:

```text
Person A:
finished ↔ software runs

Person B:
finished ↔ software + tests + documentation complete
```

Now the disagreement is easier to inspect.

The labels aligned.

The meanings did not.

---

# Different Words, Same Relationship

The opposite can happen too.

One person says:

```text
repair
```

Another says:

```text
fix
```

Another says:

```text
correct
```

Those words may correspond closely in one context.

But not every context.

The relationship must be inspected, not assumed from vocabulary alone.

---

# Alignment Needs Evidence

Suppose you propose:

```text
A ↔ B
```

Ask:

> Why?

A useful Alignment record includes evidence.

For example:

```text
Mapping:
Map marker ↔ building entrance

Evidence:
The marker's coordinates correspond to the entrance location.
```

Now another person can inspect why the relationship was proposed.

---

# Practice 6 — Mapping With Evidence

Choose one mapping:

```text
Button ↔ action

Map location ↔ physical place

Sensor ↔ measured quantity

Word ↔ definition

Requirement ↔ software behavior
```

Write:

```text
Mapping:
...

Evidence:
...

Assumptions:
...

Uncertainty:
...
```

Do not worry if the relationship is imperfect.

That may be the useful part.

---

# Assumptions Can Hold an Alignment Together

Consider:

```text
File name:
final_report.pdf
```

You might align:

```text
final_report.pdf
      ↔
final report
```

Reasonable.

But perhaps the assumption is:

```text
The filename accurately describes the current file contents.
```

That may be true.

It may not.

A mapping can depend on conventions, labels, or assumptions.

Make them visible when they matter.

---

# Practice 7 — Challenge the Label

Choose a labeled object.

For example:

```text
FINAL

APPROVED

SAFE

READY

TESTED

HEALTHY
```

Ask:

> What does the label claim to correspond to?

Then:

> What evidence would establish that relationship?

A label is not automatically proof of the state it names.

---

# Partial Alignment

Two things may correspond in some ways and not others.

Suppose you're comparing two bicycles.

Both have:

```text
Frame

Two wheels

Chain

Pedals

Brakes
```

But:

```text
Bike A:
road geometry

Bike B:
mountain geometry
```

They align structurally at one level.

They diverge at another.

---

# Practice 8 — Partial Mapping

Create:

```text
A:
...

B:
...
```

Then:

```text
Strong correspondences:
...

Partial correspondences:
...

Mismatches:
...
```

Do not force every element into one of the first two categories.

A mismatch can remain a mismatch.

---

# Conditional Alignment

Sometimes a relationship holds only under particular conditions.

For example:

```text
This map aligns with the trail system
as it existed last year.
```

The condition matters:

```text
last year's trail system
```

After a trail changes, the mapping may no longer hold completely.

Alignment can depend on:

- time;
- boundary;
- scale;
- operating condition;
- definition;
- observer position.

---

# Practice 9 — Add the Condition

Take:

```text
A corresponds to B.
```

Rewrite it as:

```text
A corresponds to B when:
...
```

or:

```text
A corresponds to B within:
...
```

or:

```text
A corresponds to B for the purpose of:
...
```

Notice how much more precise the mapping becomes.

---

# Mismatch Is Information

Suppose:

```text
Model predicts:
10

Observed:
12
```

The temptation may be to say:

> Close enough.

Maybe.

But first preserve:

```text
Prediction:
10

Observation:
12

Difference:
2
```

The mismatch may be:

- measurement noise;
- model error;
- changed conditions;
- acceptable tolerance;
- evidence of something important.

Do not erase the difference before understanding it.

---

# Practice 10 — Preserve the Difference

Consider:

```text
Expected:
20 °C

Observed:
22 °C
```

Write:

```text
Expected:
...

Observed:
...

Difference:
...

Possible explanations:
...

Acceptable tolerance:
Unknown / Known

What I would check next:
...
```

Do not decide whether the difference matters until you know what boundary and tolerance apply.

---

# Close Is Not the Same as Aligned

Two values being numerically close may or may not matter.

For example:

```text
Expected:
20.0

Observed:
20.1
```

That could be excellent Alignment.

Or disastrous.

It depends on:

- units;
- tolerance;
- application;
- measurement uncertainty;
- system requirements.

Alignment needs context.

---

# Mismatch Does Not Automatically Need Repair

This is important.

Suppose two observers disagree.

The instinct may be:

> Get them aligned.

But perhaps:

```text
Observer A
```

and:

```text
Observer B
```

have different valid access.

Their mismatch may reveal something about the system.

Before realigning, ask:

> Should these observations match?

Sometimes the answer is no.

---

# Practice 11 — Should These Match?

For each pair, ask whether strong Alignment is actually expected.

```text
User experience
vs.
server log

Map
vs.
landscape

Two thermometers
in different rooms

Two descriptions
from opposite sides of an object

Old software manual
vs.
new software behavior
```

Explain why.

The purpose is not simply to find differences.

It is to understand which differences are meaningful.

---

# Observer Alignment

Awareness showed us that observers can occupy different positions.

Alignment lets us compare their records.

Suppose:

```text
User:
Login failed.

Administrator:
Server responded normally.
```

These do not necessarily conflict.

Map the boundaries.

```text
User observation:
end-to-end login experience

Administrator observation:
server response
```

The observations may refer to different layers.

---

# Practice 12 — Map Observer Layers

Imagine:

```text
User:
Page is slow.

Application server:
Request processing is normal.

Database:
Query latency is high.
```

Create:

```text
Observer:
...

Boundary:
...

Observation:
...
```

for each.

Then ask:

> Which observations can be directly compared?

> Which describe different parts of the system?

This helps prevent false contradictions.

---

# Alignment Across Scale

A relationship may hold at one scale and fail at another.

For example:

```text
City map
 ↔
city streets
```

may be useful for navigation.

But it does not align with every:

- tree;
- pothole;
- mailbox;
- temporary road closure.

The representation was not designed for that scale.

---

# Practice 13 — Scale Check

Choose a representation:

```text
Map

Diagram

Summary

Model

Dashboard
```

Write:

```text
Designed to align with:
...

Not designed to align with:
...
```

This is a useful way to avoid demanding more correspondence than the representation promises.

---

# Alignment in Time

Two things may align at one moment and drift later.

For example:

```text
Documentation
      ↔
Software behavior
```

at release.

Several updates later:

```text
Documentation
      ↔
?
```

Nothing in the document itself has changed.

The system has.

Alignment can decay through time.

---

# Practice 14 — Check Temporal Alignment

Choose an old instruction, note, or document.

Ask:

```text
What system or condition did this originally describe?

Does that system still exist?

Which parts still align?

Which parts have drifted?

What evidence shows the change?
```

Now Alignment begins touching Continuity.

---

# Alignment and Measurements

Suppose a temperature sensor reports:

```text
30 °C
```

What does the value correspond to?

Perhaps:

```text
Air temperature near the sensor
at measurement time
```

Not necessarily:

```text
Temperature of the entire building
```

The mapping has a boundary.

---

# Practice 15 — Measurement Mapping

Given:

```text
Sensor reading:
30 °C
```

write:

```text
Measurement corresponds to:
...

Location:
...

Time:
...

Boundary:
...

What it does not automatically represent:
...
```

This prevents one local observation from silently becoming a global claim.

---

# Alignment in Data

Suppose two datasets both contain:

```text
ID
Name
Date
Value
```

They look easy to join.

But ask:

```text
Does ID mean the same thing?

Does Name use the same naming convention?

Does Date represent the same event?

Does Value use the same unit?
```

Matching column names do not guarantee matching semantics.

---

# Practice 16 — Same Schema, Different Meaning

Imagine:

```text
Dataset A:
Date = order date

Dataset B:
Date = shipment date
```

A join by Date may technically succeed.

Does it align semantically?

Not necessarily.

Create:

```text
Field:
Date

Dataset A meaning:
...

Dataset B meaning:
...

Alignment:
Strong / Partial / Misaligned

Reason:
...
```

This is a valuable systems habit.

---

# Alignment in Software Requirements 💻

A requirement may say:

```text
Users must be able to export their data.
```

The implementation provides:

```text
Download current screen as image.
```

Does that align?

Maybe not.

We need to inspect what:

```text
their data
```

and:

```text
export
```

mean in the requirement.

---

# Practice 17 — Requirement vs. Implementation

Use:

```text
Requirement:
...

Implementation:
...

Correspondence:
...

Missing behavior:
...

Extra behavior:
...

Assumptions:
...

Current Alignment:
...
```

This helps prevent:

```text
Feature exists
```

from automatically becoming:

```text
Requirement satisfied
```

---

# Alignment in Testing

A test may pass while testing the wrong thing.

Suppose:

```text
Requirement:
Reject invalid passwords.

Test:
Rejects an empty password.
```

The test aligns with one example of invalid input.

It may not align with the full requirement.

---

# Practice 18 — Test Coverage as Alignment

Ask:

```text
What requirement does this test map to?

Which part does it cover?

Which part remains untested?

Does the test name overstate what it establishes?
```

Now testing becomes an Alignment problem before it becomes a Coherence problem.

# Alignment in Research

Research often involves mapping:

```text
Concept
    ↔
Measurement
```

For example:

```text
Stress
    ↔
questionnaire score
```

or:

```text
System performance
    ↔
response time
```

The measurement is not automatically identical to the concept.

Ask:

> What part of the concept does this measurement represent?

Then:

> What does it miss?

# Practice 19 — Concept to Measurement


Now propose a measurement.

Then write:

```text

```

# Alignment and Sources

Suppose an article says:

```text
Source X proves Y.
```

Before accepting the relationship:

```text
Source X
   ↔
Claim Y
```

inspect the source.

Does it actually support Y?

Perhaps it supports:

```text
Y under condition C
```

or:

```text
a weaker version of Y
```

or:

```text
something related but different
```

Provenance depends on good Alignment.

---

# Practice 20 — Source-to-Claim Mapping

Take a source statement.

Write:

```text
Source actually says:
...

Claim being attributed:
...

Strong correspondence:
...

Difference:
...

Missing qualification:
...

Current Alignment:
...
```

The goal is not to distrust citations.

It is to make attribution inspectable.

# Alignment and AI 🤖

AI is very good at proposing correspondences.

That makes it useful.

It also makes verification important.

Try:

> Map these two frameworks, but label strong, partial, uncertain, and unsupported correspondences separately.

Then inspect the result.

---

# Ask AI for Counter-Alignment

Another useful prompt is:

> Give me the strongest reasonable argument that these two things do not correspond.

Now compare:

```text
Proposed Mapping
      ↕
Counter-Mapping
```

Ask:

> Which evidence supports each?

You don't need to choose immediately.

The disagreement reveals assumptions.

---

# AI Can Over-Align

AI may see patterns everywhere.

It may map:

```text
A
 ↔
B
```

because both have three parts.

Or similar vocabulary.

Or similar themes.

That is not enough by itself.

Ask:

> What relationship makes this mapping meaningful beyond superficial similarity?

This is especially important when comparing theories, models, or historical materials.

---

# Similarity Is Not Provenance

Two ideas can resemble one another without one causing or influencing the other.

A structural resemblance establishes:

```text
similarity
```

not automatically:

```text
historical influence
```

To claim influence, you need evidence about lineage.

Alignment should not silently become provenance.

---

# Practice 21 — Similarity vs. Influence

Imagine two documents contain similar three-part structures.

Write:

```text
Structural similarity:
...

Possible historical relationship:
...

Evidence of influence:
...

Unknown:
...
```

If evidence of influence is absent, leave it absent.

That's disciplined Alignment.

# Alignment and Formal Reasoning

Formal equality gives us a particularly strict example of correspondence.

If Lean knows:

```text
a = b
```

then that relationship can justify specific rewriting.

The key lesson is not merely:

```text
Use rw.
```

It is:

> A declared or proven relationship permits the substitution.

The correspondence comes first.

# Practice 22 — Formal Alignment Without Lean

Given:

```text
a = b
```

consider:

```text
a + 1

b + 1
```

Ask:

> What relationship justifies treating these expressions as corresponding?

Then:

> What would happen if a and b were merely similar rather than equal?

You've started thinking formally about Alignment.

# Mapping Direction Matters

Suppose:

```text
Every A maps to one B.
```

Does that mean:

```text
Every B maps to one A?
```

Not necessarily.

Relationships can have direction.

For example:

```text
Employee
   ↓
Department
```

may assign each employee to a department.

That does not mean each department corresponds to only one employee.

---

# Practice 23 — Reverse the Arrow

Take a mapping:

```text
A → B
```

Ask:

> Does B → A also hold?

For example:

```text
City → Country
```

Reverse:

```text
Country → City
```

The relationship changed.

Direction is part of Alignment.

---

# One-to-One, One-to-Many, Many-to-One

Mappings can have different shapes.

```text
One-to-one

A → X
B → Y
C → Z
```

```text
Many-to-one

A ┐
B ├→ X
C ┘
```

```text
One-to-many

A → X
  → Y
  → Z
```

These structures matter.

A mapping is more than a line between labels.

---

# Practice 24 — Identify the Shape

Choose:

```text
Students → Class

Book → Authors

Country → Cities

Username → Account

File extension → Possible file formats
```

Ask whether the relationship is:

```text
One-to-one

One-to-many

Many-to-one

Many-to-many
```

Then ask whether your answer depends on the chosen system boundary.

# Alignment Can Be Asymmetric

Perhaps:

```text
A is a useful representation of B
```

but:

```text
B is not a useful representation of A
```

For example, a summary may represent a long report for a particular purpose.

The full report is not simply a representation of the summary.

Direction and purpose matter.

---

# Alignment and Loss

Mapping one structure into another may lose information.

For example:

```text
Detailed map
      ↓
Simplified route diagram
```

The route diagram may preserve:

```text
Order of stops
```

while losing:

```text
Exact distance

Street geometry

Nearby landmarks
```

Alignment can include loss.

The useful question is:

> Did we preserve what mattered for the purpose?

---

# Practice 25 — What Was Lost?

Take:

```text
Original:
Detailed paragraph

Representation:
Three-bullet summary
```

Ask:

```text
What corresponded?

What was removed?

What meaning was preserved?

What nuance was lost?

Was the loss acceptable for the purpose?
```

This bridges naturally into Clarity.

# Alignment States

For practice, it can be useful to describe an Alignment using words such as:

```text
Strong

Partial

Conditional

Uncertain

Misaligned

Unmapped
```

These are not scores.

They are descriptions of the current relationship.

---

# Strong Alignment

Use:

```text
Strong
```

when the important relationships in the chosen boundary correspond well and are supported.

That does not mean:

```text
identical
```

It means the mapping is doing its intended job.

---

# Partial Alignment

Use:

```text
Partial
```

when meaningful correspondences exist but important differences remain.

This is common.

Partial Alignment is not failure.

Sometimes it is the most accurate description.

---

# Conditional Alignment

Use:

```text
Conditional
```

when the mapping holds under stated conditions.

For example:

```text
This documentation aligns with software version 2.1.
```

The condition belongs to the judgment.

---

# Uncertain Alignment

Use:

```text
Uncertain
```

when the relationship may exist but available evidence is insufficient.

Do not upgrade uncertainty merely because a mapping would be convenient.

---

# Misaligned

Use:

```text
Misaligned
```

when important relationships conflict within the chosen boundary.

The mismatch itself may tell you what needs investigation.

---

# Unmapped

Sometimes:

```text
No mapping has been established.
```

That is different from:

```text
The mapping is false.
```

Keep the distinction.

# Alignment Self-Check

Before moving to Mastery, ask:

```text
Can I identify what I am comparing?

Can I state the comparison boundary?

Can I propose correspondences?

Can I explain why they are meaningful?

Can I identify assumptions?

Can I preserve mismatches?

Can I recognize partial or conditional Alignment?

Can I distinguish similarity from provenance?

Can I identify unmapped elements?

Can I leave the relationship uncertain when evidence is insufficient?
```

If most of those answers are yes, you are practicing Alignment.

# Before Mastery

One last reminder.

Alignment is not:

```text
Make A and B match.
```

It is not:

```text
Find similarities.
```

It is not:

```text
Repair every mismatch.
```

Alignment is closer to:

> Make the relationship between A and B inspectable enough to see what corresponds, what does not, and under what conditions.

Sometimes the best result is:

```text
Strong correspondence here.

Partial correspondence there.

Important mismatch here.

Unknown relationship there.
```

That is useful.
