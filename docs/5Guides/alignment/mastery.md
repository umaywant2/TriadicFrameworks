
# Alignment Mastery

> What corresponds?

You have explored Alignment.

You have practiced:

- identifying what is being compared;
- defining comparison boundaries;
- proposing correspondences;
- supporting mappings with evidence;
- exposing assumptions;
- preserving mismatches;
- recognizing partial and conditional Alignment;
- distinguishing similarity from identity;
- distinguishing resemblance from provenance;
- and leaving relationships unresolved when the evidence is insufficient.

Now it is time to use the skill on a fresh problem.

The goal is not to make two things agree.

The goal is:

> Make their relationship visible enough to show what corresponds, what does not, and why.

---

# Before You Begin

Bring Awareness with you.

Before asking:

> What corresponds?

ask:

> What am I actually comparing?

A weak observation does not become stronger because it maps neatly onto something else.

Your sequence is:

```text
Awareness
    ↓
Alignment
```

First establish the objects.

Then inspect the relationship.

---

# The Mastery Challenge

Imagine you are evaluating a small bicycle repair guide.

The guide says:

```text
SYMPTOM:
The chain skips when the rider pedals hard.

POSSIBLE CAUSE:
The rear derailleur needs adjustment.

RECOMMENDED ACTION:
Adjust the rear derailleur.
```

Now inspect the bicycle.

You observe:

```text
The chain skips under heavy pedaling.

The rear derailleur appears visually aligned.

The chain shows visible wear.

Several rear sprocket teeth also show wear.

The bicycle shifts normally during light pedaling.

You have not measured chain wear.

You have not tested derailleur adjustment.

You have not inspected every drivetrain component.
```

The guide looks relevant.

The bicycle's symptom appears to match.

But how far does the Alignment actually go?

---

# Don't Diagnose the Bicycle Yet

You may already be thinking:

> It's the worn chain.

Maybe.

Or:

> The derailleur only looks aligned.

Also possible.

Or:

> The guide is wrong.

Maybe not.

The mastery challenge is not bicycle diagnosis.

It is Alignment.

Your task is to inspect the relationship between:

```text
Repair Guide
      ↕
Observed Bicycle
```

without forcing either one to fit the other.

---

# Step 1 — Awareness

Start with the two objects.

## Object A

```text
Repair Guide
```

The guide provides:

```text
Symptom:
Chain skips when pedaling hard.

Possible cause:
Rear derailleur needs adjustment.

Recommended action:
Adjust rear derailleur.
```

## Object B

```text
Observed Bicycle
```

The current observations include:

```text
Chain skips under heavy pedaling.

Rear derailleur appears visually aligned.

Chain shows visible wear.

Some sprocket teeth show visible wear.

Shifting is normal under light pedaling.
```

And several things remain untested.

Keep those unknowns visible.

---

# Step 2 — Define the Comparison Boundary

What exactly are you comparing?

A poor boundary might be:

```text
Repair guide
      ↕
Bicycle
```

That's too broad.

A more useful boundary might be:

```text
Guide symptom, cause, and recommended action

compared with

Observed drivetrain behavior and visible condition
```

Now the comparison becomes more precise.

---

# Boundary Record

Write:

```text
COMPARISON BOUNDARY

I am comparing:
...

I am not currently comparing:
...
```

For example:

```text
I am comparing:
The guide's stated symptom and proposed cause with
the observed drivetrain behavior and visible condition.

I am not currently comparing:
Every possible cause of chain skipping or the complete
condition of the bicycle.
```

That keeps the task manageable.

---

# Step 3 — Map the Symptom

The strongest initial mapping may be:

```text
Guide:
Chain skips when rider pedals hard.

Observed Bicycle:
Chain skips under heavy pedaling.
```

That looks like a strong correspondence.

Record it.

```text
Mapping:
Guide symptom ↔ observed symptom

Evidence:
Both describe chain skipping under increased pedaling load.
```

Good.

Now stop.

A strong symptom match does not automatically establish a cause match.

---

# Symptom Alignment Is Not Cause Alignment

This is one of the central lessons of the challenge.

We have:

```text
Guide Symptom
      ↕
Observed Symptom
```

That does not automatically give us:

```text
Guide Cause
      ↕
Actual Cause
```

The second mapping needs its own support.

Do not let the strength of the first mapping leak into the second.

---

# Step 4 — Map the Proposed Cause

The guide proposes:

```text
Rear derailleur needs adjustment.
```

Current observation:

```text
Rear derailleur appears visually aligned.
```

Does that refute the guide?

Not necessarily.

"Appears visually aligned" may not establish:

```text
Adjustment is correct under load.
```

But it also does not positively support the guide's proposed cause.

A careful current mapping might be:

```text
Guide cause:
Rear derailleur needs adjustment.

Observed evidence:
No direct confirmation.

Current relationship:
Uncertain.
```

That is a legitimate Alignment result.

---

# Step 5 — Preserve Competing Evidence

The bicycle also shows:

```text
Visible chain wear

Visible sprocket wear
```

Those observations do not yet establish a cause either.

But they matter because they introduce relationships not represented in the guide's short explanation.

Your comparison now looks more like:

```text
Guide
├── Symptom:
│   chain skips under load
│
├── Possible cause:
│   derailleur adjustment
│
└── Action:
    adjust derailleur


Observed Bicycle
├── Symptom:
│   chain skips under load
│
├── Derailleur:
│   visually appears aligned
│
├── Chain:
│   visible wear
│
└── Sprockets:
    visible wear
```

The symptom strongly corresponds.

The cause does not yet.

The observed system also contains information outside the guide's immediate model.

---

# Step 6 — Preserve the Mismatch

Do not hide:

```text
Guide focuses on derailleur adjustment.
```

while:

```text
Observed bicycle contains visible wear elsewhere.
```

That difference is useful.

Record it:

```text
MISMATCH

The guide's possible-cause model focuses on derailleur
adjustment.

The observed bicycle also contains visible drivetrain wear
that the guide excerpt does not address.
```

Notice what this does not say:

> Therefore the guide is wrong.

It only preserves the difference.

---

# Step 7 — Identify Unmapped Elements

Some elements do not currently have partners.

For example:

```text
Observed:
Visible chain wear

Guide partner:
None stated
```

and:

```text
Observed:
Visible sprocket wear

Guide partner:
None stated
```

Those are unmapped elements.

They should not be forced into:

```text
derailleur adjustment
```

simply because that is the cause available in the guide.

---

# Unmapped Does Not Mean Irrelevant

An unmapped observation may turn out to be:

- important;
- irrelevant;
- another symptom;
- another cause;
- a consequence;
- unrelated background information.

You do not know yet.

The Alignment task is simply:

> This observation does not currently map into the guide's stated explanation.

That is useful.

---

# Step 8 — Examine the Recommended Action

The guide recommends:

```text
Adjust the rear derailleur.
```

Does the observed symptom align with the guide's trigger for that action?

Partly.

But does the available evidence establish that the action addresses the observed cause?

Not yet.

Separate:

```text
Symptom correspondence
```

from:

```text
Action justification
```

The first may be strong.

The second may remain conditional or uncertain.

---

# Step 9 — Identify the Assumptions

For the guide's recommendation to align strongly with the observed bicycle, several assumptions may need to hold.

For example:

```text
The skipping is caused by derailleur adjustment.

The visual appearance does not rule out an adjustment problem.

The visible drivetrain wear is not the primary cause.

The guide's symptom description applies to this drivetrain.
```

You do not need to prove or reject those assumptions yet.

Make them visible.

---

# Step 10 — Identify the Unknowns

The scenario explicitly tells you:

```text
Chain wear has not been measured.

Derailleur adjustment has not been tested.

Not every drivetrain component has been inspected.
```

Good.

Preserve those unknowns.

Do not convert:

```text
not tested
```

into:

```text
probably fine
```

or:

```text
probably broken
```

Unknown means unknown.

---

# Step 11 — Choose the Next Alignment Check

Now ask:

> What additional observation would most help evaluate the proposed cause mapping?

Possible checks might concern:

- measured chain wear;
- derailleur behavior under relevant conditions;
- drivetrain wear;
- another part of the drivetrain.

The mastery challenge does not require performing a bicycle repair.

The skill is choosing information that can strengthen, weaken, or distinguish the mappings.

---

# A Good Next Observation Has a Purpose

Suppose you want to evaluate:

```text
Guide cause
      ↕
Observed bicycle
```

Then the next observation should help test that relationship.

Randomly collecting more bicycle information may not help.

Alignment becomes useful when the next observation has a job.

# A Possible Current Judgment

A strong response might conclude something like:

```text
Current Alignment:
Partial.

Reason:
The bicycle's chain-skipping symptom aligns strongly with
the guide's symptom description.

The guide's proposed derailleur-adjustment cause does not
yet have equivalent support from the available observations.

Visible chain and sprocket wear are currently unmapped to
the guide's explanation.

Additional observation is needed before the guide's
recommended action can be strongly aligned with the
observed problem.
```

Notice what this judgment does.

It preserves the useful match.

It preserves the mismatch.

It preserves uncertainty.

It does not diagnose the bicycle.

---

# What Would Be Too Strong?

For example:

```text
The guide matches the symptom, so the derailleur is the problem.
```

That promotes:

```text
symptom Alignment
```

into:

```text
cause Alignment
```

without enough evidence.

---

# Another Too-Strong Result

```text
The derailleur looks aligned, so the guide is wrong.
```

That may also go too far.

The current observation does not necessarily establish that every relevant derailleur condition is correct.

Again:

> Preserve what the observation actually supports.

---

# What Would Be Too Weak?

Consider:

```text
The guide and bicycle are somewhat related.
```

Safe.

Not very useful.

A stronger Alignment analysis says where the relationship is strong, where it is weak, and what remains unmapped.

---

# Mastery Lives Between the Extremes

We are looking for:

```text
Forced Match
     ↓

Inspectable Mapping
+
Evidence
+
Mismatch
+
Uncertainty

     ↓
Vague Nonjudgment
```

Alignment Mastery lives in the middle.

# Mastery Criteria

You are demonstrating Alignment when you can substantially do the following.

## Identify the Objects

Can you clearly state what is being compared?

## Define the Boundary

Can you identify which aspects are included in the comparison?

## Propose Correspondences

Can you identify meaningful mappings?

## Support the Mapping

Can you explain why those correspondences are proposed?

## Separate Layers

Can you distinguish:

```text
symptom Alignment
```

from:

```text
cause Alignment
```

or other distinct relationships?

## Preserve Mismatch

Can you leave important differences visible?

## Identify Unmapped Elements

Can you recognize observations for which the comparison provides no current partner?

## Expose Assumptions

Can you identify conditions the mapping depends upon?

## Preserve Unknowns

Can you leave untested relationships unresolved?

## Qualify the Judgment

Can you describe the relationship as strong, partial, conditional, uncertain, misaligned, or unmapped where appropriate?

---

# What Mastery Does Not Require

You do not need to:

- diagnose the bicycle;
- repair the bicycle;
- prove the guide correct;
- prove the guide wrong;
- eliminate every uncertainty;
- use formal terminology;
- use Lean;
- use AI;
- reach the same judgment as another learner.

The mastery target is:

> Can you make the relationship between the guide and the observed bicycle inspectable?

# A Useful Retry

If your first answer immediately diagnoses the bicycle, remove the diagnosis.

Keep only:

```text
Observed

Mapped

Unmapped

Assumed

Unknown
```

Then rebuild the Alignment judgment from those pieces.

---

# Another Retry

If your first answer says:

```text
Nothing aligns because we do not know the cause.
```

look again.

The symptom itself may align strongly.

Alignment does not require the entire structures to match before any useful correspondence can exist.

Preserve partial relationships.

# Bridge to Coherence

You now have an Alignment Record.

You know:

```text
What you compared

Which mappings are strong

Which are partial

Which depend on conditions

Where mismatches remain

Which elements are unmapped

What remains uncertain
```

Excellent.

Now something interesting happens.

Suppose several individual mappings seem reasonable.

Do they work together?

Can all of the relationships hold at the same time?

Does the complete structure make sense?

That gives us the next question:

> Does this hold together?

That is Coherence.

---

# Don't Throw Alignment Away

When Coherence exposes a contradiction, return here.

Ask:

> Did I force a mapping?

> Did two labels hide different meanings?

> Was the comparison boundary wrong?

> Did a relationship drift?

The Guides build forward.

They also let you move backward when needed.
