# Continuity

> What persists through change?

You have reached the fifth Guide.

So far, you have practiced four questions:

```text
Awareness
What am I observing?

Alignment
What corresponds?

Coherence
Does this hold together?

Clarity
Can this be understood and inspected?
```

Now we add one more:

> What persists through change?

Welcome to Continuity.

---

# Everything Changes

Systems change.

Documents change.

Software changes.

Ideas change.

People learn.

Objects wear out and get repaired.

Patterns repeat, drift, break, and sometimes return.

Continuity does not ask us to stop those changes.

It asks:

> What happened between there and here?

---

# The Basic Shape

Continuity begins with a simple structure:

```text
Before
   ↓
Change
   ↓
After
```

We can immediately ask:

> What changed?

Then:

> What stayed the same?

Then:

> What relationship connects the earlier state to the later one?

That relationship is where Continuity becomes interesting.

---

# Continuity Is Not Sameness

Consider a bicycle.

Replace a tire.

Still the same bicycle?

Probably.

Replace the chain.

Repair the brakes.

Change the seat.

The bicycle has changed.

Yet something still connects its earlier and later states.

So Continuity does not mean:

> Nothing changed.

It means:

> Enough structure remains, or enough history is preserved, that we can inspect how one state relates to another.

---

# Same in What Way?

When we say:

> It's the same.

we may mean several different things.

For example:

```text
Same physical object

Same function

Same owner

Same name

Same history

Same structure

Same purpose

Same meaning
```

One kind of continuity may survive while another does not.

That gives us an important question:

> Continuity of what?

---

# From State to History

Earlier Guides often focused on what is present now.

Continuity adds history.

Instead of:

```text
Current State
```

we can inspect:

```text
Earlier State
      ↓
Transition
      ↓
Current State
```

And sometimes:

```text
State₀
  ↓
State₁
  ↓
State₂
  ↓
State₃
```

The current state tells us where we are.

The history may help explain how we got here.

---

# Change

A change can be:

- sudden;
- gradual;
- expected;
- unexpected;
- intentional;
- accidental;
- useful;
- harmful;
- reversible;
- irreversible;
- or simply not understood yet.

Do not begin by deciding what kind of change it is.

First ask:

> What actually changed?

Awareness still comes first.

---

# Persistence

Something may remain despite change.

For example:

```text
Old tire
   ↓
Replacement
   ↓
New tire
```

The physical tire changed.

But perhaps these persisted:

```text
Location on bicycle

Functional role

Relationship to wheel

Purpose
```

Continuity can exist through preserved relationships even when particular components change.

---

# Transition

A transition connects two states.

```text
Before
   ↓
Transition
   ↓
After
```

A useful transition description might include:

```text
Before:
What was true?

Change:
What happened?

After:
What is now true?

Persisted:
What remained?

Unknown:
What don't we know?
```

Now the change leaves a trail.

---

# Drift

Not every change happens at once.

Sometimes:

```text
A
 ↓
A'
 ↓
A''
 ↓
A'''
 ↓
B
```

Each change may look small.

But eventually the latest state differs significantly from the earlier one.

That is drift.

---

# Drift Is Not Automatically Bad

Suppose your understanding changes because new evidence arrives.

You once thought:

```text
A always causes B.
```

Later:

```text
A may contribute to B under condition C.
```

Your understanding drifted from the original.

But the newer version may be better.

So when drift appears, do not immediately ask:

> How do I restore the original?

Ask:

> Why did this change?

Then:

> Was the changed part supposed to persist?

---

# Drift Can Also Hide Problems

Now consider:

```text
Version 1:
Preserve uncertainty.

Version 2:
Resolve uncertainty when possible.

Version 3:
Prefer a confident conclusion.

Version 4:
Give a confident answer.
```

No single change looks enormous.

But the original principle has disappeared.

History makes that easier to see.

---

# Recurring Patterns

Once we preserve history, we may discover repetition.

For example:

```text
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
 ↓
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
```

We can safely observe:

> The sequence A → B → C occurred more than once.

That does not automatically mean:

> A causes B.

or:

> C causes A.

Recurrence is an observation.

Causation is another claim.

---

# Broken Patterns

Now imagine:

```text
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
 ↓
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
D
```

The familiar pattern broke.

Do not replace D with C simply because C was expected.

Instead:

```text
Expected:
C

Observed:
D
```

Then investigate.

A broken pattern may contain more useful information than another successful repetition.

---

# Unexpected Does Not Mean Impossible

Suppose your transition model expects:

```text
A
 ↓
B
```

but you observe:

```text
A
 ↓
D
```

A careful conclusion is:

> This transition was unexpected under the current model.

Not:

> This transition is impossible.

The observation may be wrong.

The model may be incomplete.

An intermediate state may be missing.

The system may have changed.

You do not know yet.

Preserve the uncertainty.

---

# Current State Is Not the Whole Story

Consider two systems.

## System A

```text
NORMAL
  ↓
NORMAL
  ↓
NORMAL
```

## System B

```text
NORMAL
  ↓
PROBLEM
  ↓
RECOVERY
  ↓
NORMAL
```

Both currently report:

```text
NORMAL
```

But they do not have the same history.

If the history matters to the problem, storing only the latest state loses useful information.

---

# Provenance

Continuity and provenance work naturally together.

Provenance asks:

> Where did this come from?

Continuity asks:

> How did it get from there to here?

Together:

```text
Source
  ↓
Change
  ↓
Revision
  ↓
Current State
```

Now the path itself becomes inspectable.

---

# Continuity in Documents

Imagine a research statement begins as:

```text
The result may support the proposed explanation.
```

A revision changes it to:

```text
The result supports the proposed explanation.
```

Later:

```text
The result confirms the explanation.
```

The writing became more confident.

But did the evidence change?

If not, the meaning may have drifted.

Continuity helps us compare the claim across revisions.

---

# Continuity in Software

Imagine software evolves:

```text
v1
 ↓
v2
 ↓
v3
```

Some things may intentionally change:

```text
Interface
Implementation
Performance
Features
```

Other things may be expected to persist:

```text
User data

Security boundaries

Public contracts

Documented behavior

Privacy promises
```

The important question is not:

> Did the software change?

Of course it did.

The useful question is:

> What was supposed to remain stable?

---

# Continuity in Learning

Learning should contain change.

A healthy learning path might look like:

```text
Initial Understanding
        ↓
Observation
        ↓
Challenge
        ↓
Revision
        ↓
Better Question
```

If your understanding changes when new evidence appears, that may be exactly what should happen.

Continuity preserves enough history to understand the revision.

---

# Continuity in Conversation

Long conversations can drift too.

You may begin with:

```text
Goal A
```

and twenty turns later discover that you are working on:

```text
Goal B
```

That may be intentional.

Or not.

A useful checkpoint is:

```text
Original goal:
...

Current goal:
...

What changed:
...

Why:
...

Still relevant:
...

Next step:
...
```

That makes conversational drift visible.

---

# Continuity and Repair

Suppose something breaks.

The instinct may be:

> Restore the previous state.

Sometimes that is correct.

Sometimes the earlier state contained the problem.

Before restoring anything, ask:

> What should actually persist?

Then:

> What should change?

Repair is easier to reason about when the continuity target is clear.

---

# Continuity and Learning From Failure

A failed transition can be useful.

Suppose:

```text
Expected
   ↓
A → B → C
```

but:

```text
Observed
   ↓
A → B → D
```

Instead of hiding the break, preserve it.

Then ask:

- What changed?
- What assumptions predicted C?
- What evidence produced D?
- Is something missing?
- Does the model need revision?

Failure becomes evidence.

---

# Continuity of Meaning

One of the hardest things to track is meaning.

A label can remain unchanged while its definition changes.

For example:

```text
Alignment v1
```

and:

```text
Alignment v2
```

may use the same word.

But if the underlying definition changed, the meaning may not be continuous.

That is semantic drift.

Continuity asks us to track meaning, not only labels.

---

# Continuity of Structure

Sometimes the values change while the structure persists.

For example:

```text
State₀
A = 3
B = 6
C = 9
```

becomes:

```text
State₁
A = 4
B = 7
C = 10
```

All three values changed.

But the structure:

```text
A
B
C
```

and the mapping:

```text
A₀ ↔ A₁
B₀ ↔ B₁
C₀ ↔ C₁
```

remain.

This is structural continuity.

---

# Continuity of Function

An implementation can change while its function remains.

For example:

```text
Old implementation
       ↓
Replacement
       ↓
New implementation
```

If both preserve the required behavior, we may have functional continuity.

But that depends on what behavior was required.

Again:

> Continuity of what?

---

# Continuity of Commitment

Some relationships are promises.

For example:

> Learners can explore the Guides without being required to identify themselves.

If a future implementation changes, ask:

> Does this commitment still hold?

A feature change that quietly breaks an earlier promise is a Continuity issue.

This is why privacy needs Continuity too.

---

# What Should Persist?

Useful candidates may include:

- meaning;
- safety;
- privacy;
- attribution;
- identity;
- function;
- relationships;
- interfaces;
- evidence;
- history;
- goals;
- assumptions that remain justified.

Different systems preserve different things.

Continuity requires knowing what matters.

---

# What Should Not Persist?

Some things should disappear:

- errors;
- obsolete assumptions;
- insecure behavior;
- unsupported claims;
- accidental complexity;
- broken relationships;
- misleading terminology.

Continuity does not mean preserving mistakes because they are old.

Sometimes the correct history is:

```text
Earlier State
contained problem X

Evidence
showed X was wrong

Revision
removed X

Current State
no longer contains X
```

That is healthy change.

---

# The Core Question Gets More Precise

We began with:

> What persists through change?

Now we can make it more specific:

> What was expected to persist?

> What actually persisted?

> What changed?

> Why did it change?

> What relationship connects the earlier and later states?

> What evidence supports that history?

> What remains uncertain?

That is the heart of Continuity.

Continuity is the fifth Guide.

You still need them.

---

# Awareness Inside Continuity

Before comparing states:

> What did I actually observe in each state?

A difference in observation method can look like a difference in the system.

Awareness helps us notice that.

---

# Alignment Inside Continuity

Between states:

> What corresponds?

Perhaps:

```text
A₀ ↔ A₁
B₀ ↔ B₁
C₀ ↔ ?
```

Now we can see persistence, change, and missing relationships.

---

# Coherence Inside Continuity

Across a history:

> Does the explanation of the transitions hold together?

Several individually plausible changes may produce an incoherent overall history.

Coherence helps expose that.

---

# Clarity Inside Continuity

Finally:

> Can another person inspect what changed?

A useful history makes visible:

```text
Before
Change
After
Persisted
Broken
Unknown
```

Clarity makes Continuity usable.

# One Last Thought Before You Begin

Continuity is easy to misunderstand as:

> Keep things the same.

It isn't.

A better description is:

> Preserve enough structure and history to understand what changed and why the present still relates to the past.

Sometimes the correct result is preservation.

Sometimes it is adaptation.

Sometimes it is correction.

Sometimes it is an intentional break.

And sometimes the most honest answer is:

> We don't yet know what happened between these states.

That's still useful.
