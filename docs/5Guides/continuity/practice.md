# Continuity Practice

> What persists through change?

You have already explored an important Continuity idea:

Things can change without becoming completely disconnected from what came before.

A system can move through many states.

A learner can revise an understanding.

Software can be updated.

A pattern can recur, drift, break, and return.

Continuity gives us a way to inspect that history.

In this practice section, we will move through three levels:

```text
Everyday Change
      ↓
System Change
      ↓
Formal Change
```

The goal is not to prevent change.

The goal is to become better at seeing what change does.

---

# Start With a Simple Question

Look at something that has changed recently.

It might be:

- a room;
- a bicycle;
- a recipe;
- a piece of software;
- a schedule;
- a document;
- a skill you are learning;
- or something else familiar.

Ask:

> What is different now?

Then ask the harder question:

> What is still recognizably the same?

That second question begins Continuity.

---

# Before, Change, After

A useful starting structure is:

```text
Before
   ↓
Change
   ↓
After
```

For example:

```text
Bicycle
Chain slipping
   ↓
Adjustment
   ↓
Bicycle
Chain running correctly
```

Something changed.

But many things persisted:

- it is still the same bicycle;
- most components are unchanged;
- the chain still performs the same general role;
- the rider still expects pedaling to drive the wheel.

Continuity does not require sameness.

It asks how the earlier and later states remain connected.

---

# Practice 1 — Three Columns

Choose a small everyday change.

Create three columns:

```text
BEFORE        CHANGE        AFTER

______        ______        ______

______        ______        ______

______        ______        ______
```

Now ask:

> Which details changed?

> Which details persisted?

> Which changed but retained the same role?

> Did anything disappear?

> Did anything new appear?

Try not to decide whether the change was good or bad yet.

First make the transition visible.

---

# Change Is Not the Same as Loss

Suppose you replace a worn bicycle tire.

Before:

```text
Old tire
Worn tread
Still mounted on wheel
```

After:

```text
New tire
Fresh tread
Mounted on same wheel
```

The tire changed.

The bicycle did not cease to be the bicycle.

The wheel did not cease to be the wheel.

The function of the tire remained recognizable.

So we might describe the transition as:

```text
Component changed
      +
Role persisted
      +
Larger system persisted
```

That is one kind of Continuity.

---

# Identity Can Be Layered

Now make the example harder.

Imagine gradually replacing:

- the tires;
- the chain;
- the brakes;
- the seat;
- the handlebars.

At what point is it no longer the same bicycle?

There may not be one simple answer.

That's useful.

Continuity may depend on which layer you are examining:

```text
Physical components
Functional role
Ownership history
Configuration
Identity label
Use
```

Different layers may preserve different kinds of continuity.

# Practice 2 — Name the Layer

Choose something that changed.

Then complete:

```text
Physical continuity:
...

Functional continuity:
...

...

Meaning or identity continuity:
...
```

You may find that one layer persists while another does not.

That is more informative than simply saying:

> It changed.

# A Learning Example

Suppose you believe:

```text
A causes B.
```

Then you learn something new.

Your understanding becomes:

```text
A may contribute to B,
but only under condition C.
```

Did your understanding break?

Maybe not.

Part of the earlier idea persisted.

Part changed.

A useful Continuity record might be:

```text
Before:
A causes B.

New information:
Condition C matters.

After:
A may contribute to B when C holds.

Persisted:
A and B remain related.

Changed:
The relationship is no longer unconditional.
```

That is learning with provenance.

---

# Don't Hide the Earlier Version

It can be tempting to rewrite your notes so they look as though you always understood the final version.

Don't.

The transition itself may be useful.

```text
Earlier understanding
       ↓
New evidence
       ↓
Revised understanding
```

Now you can inspect how learning happened.

That history may later help you understand another change.

---

# Practice 3 — Track a Change in Understanding

Choose something you once understood differently.

It does not have to be profound.

Write:

```text
I used to think:
...

Then I noticed or learned:
...

Now I think:
...

What persisted:
...

What changed:
...

What remains uncertain:
...
```

You've just created a small Continuity record.

# Drift

Not every change happens all at once.

Sometimes:

```text
State₀
  ↓
small change
  ↓
State₁
  ↓
small change
  ↓
State₂
  ↓
small change
  ↓
State₃
```

Each transition looks minor.

But eventually:

```text
State₀ ≠ State₃
```

in some important way.

We call this drift.

---

# Practice 4 — Spot the Drift

Imagine instructions originally say:

> Keep the explanation short and preserve uncertainty.

Version 2 becomes:

> Keep the explanation short and resolve uncertainty when possible.

Version 3 becomes:

> Give a short confident answer.

Version 4 becomes:

> Give the answer confidently.

No single transition looks enormous.

But compare the endpoints.

The original instruction:

```text
preserve uncertainty
```

has disappeared.

Ask:

> At which transition did the meaning begin to drift?

> Which change looked harmless by itself?

> What should have been preserved?

That is Continuity inspection.

---

# Drift Is Not Automatically Wrong

Now imagine another sequence:

```text
Original explanation
      ↓
New evidence
      ↓
Revision
      ↓
Better explanation
```

The result drifted away from the original.

But perhaps it should have.

So don't ask only:

> Did this change?

Ask:

> Why did it change?

Then:

> Was the changed part supposed to persist?

Drift can indicate:

- degradation;
- correction;
- adaptation;
- learning;
- new evidence;
- changing conditions;
- or a changed boundary.

Inspect before judging.

---

# When to Preserve

Preservation matters when something is still required.

Examples might include:

- an important safety constraint;
- the meaning of a term;
- a documented interface contract;
- a mathematical invariant;
- a privacy promise;
- a source attribution;
- a required behavior.

If later changes quietly remove one of those, we may have a Continuity problem.

---

# When Not to Preserve

Something should not remain merely because it was there first.

An earlier state may contain:

- an error;
- a weak assumption;
- an obsolete requirement;
- an inaccurate model;
- an unnecessary restriction.

Continuity is not stubbornness.

Sometimes the honest continuity record is:

```text
Earlier rule:
X

Evidence:
X was unsupported.

Change:
Removed X.

Persisted:
The broader goal remained.

Reason:
The model improved.
```

That is healthy change.

---

# Practice 5 — Should It Persist?

For each item, decide whether persistence is probably important, optional, or undesirable.

```text
A known software defect

A safety requirement

An outdated assumption

A public API contract

A typo

A privacy promise

An unsupported theory claim

A useful historical record
```

Then explain why.

The purpose is not to produce one universal answer.

The purpose is to notice that different structures deserve different continuity expectations.

# Systems Practice

Now move from everyday examples into a simple system.

Imagine a login service with states such as:

```text
NORMAL

PLAUSIBLE_DEGRADATION

HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE

DIFFERENT_PATTERN

INSUFFICIENT_DATA

UNCERTAIN
```

The current state tells us something.

But history may tell us more.

---

# State Is Not History

Consider:

```text
Current State:
NORMAL
```

That looks reassuring.

Now compare two histories.

## System A

```text
NORMAL
  ↓
NORMAL
  ↓
NORMAL
```

## System B

```text
NORMAL
  ↓
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
  ↓
UNCERTAIN
  ↓
NORMAL
```

Both are currently normal.

They do not have the same history.

Continuity asks us to preserve that distinction when it matters.

---

# Practice 6 — Compare the Histories

Ask:

> Which system changed?

> Which returned to an earlier state?

> Which experienced uncertainty?

> What would be lost if we stored only the current state?

> Would the history matter for your next investigation?

This is why transition history can be useful.

---

# Transition

A transition is the relationship between states.

```text
Previous State
      ↓
Transition
      ↓
New State
```

A useful transition record might include:

```text
Before:
NORMAL

Observation:
response time increasing

After:
PLAUSIBLE_DEGRADATION
```

Now we know more than:

```text
Current State:
PLAUSIBLE_DEGRADATION
```

We have a path.

---

# Practice 7 — Explain the Transition

Given:

```text
Before:
NORMAL

New observation:
request volume increasing
response time increasing
failed logins increasing

After:
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
```

Ask:

> What changed?

> Which observations support the transition?

> What remains uncertain?

> Is the new state a diagnosis or a current interpretation?

> What observation might change it again?

This brings Awareness, Alignment, and Coherence into Continuity.

---

# Transition Validation

Sometimes systems declare which transitions they expect.

For example:

```text
NORMAL
   ↓
PLAUSIBLE_DEGRADATION
```

may be expected.

But:

```text
DIFFERENT_PATTERN
   ↓
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
```

might not currently exist in the transition model.

That does not automatically mean the transition is impossible.

It means:

> The observed transition does not currently align with the model.

That is something to inspect.

---

# Don't Rewrite History to Protect the Model

Suppose the system observed:

```text
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
D
```

but your model expected:

```text
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
```

Do not quietly replace D with C.

That would make the history look coherent by discarding the evidence that challenged it.

Instead record:

```text
Expected:
C

Observed:
D

Status:
Pattern break
```

Then investigate.

---

# Unexpected Transitions Can Teach Us

An unexpected transition may mean:

- the system changed;
- the environment changed;
- an observation is missing;
- the earlier state was incorrect;
- the transition model is incomplete;
- or something genuinely new occurred.

The first task is not repair.

The first task is inspection.

---

# Recurring Patterns

Once the history becomes longer, repeated sequences may emerge.

For example:

```text
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
 ↓
A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C
```

Now we can say:

> The sequence A → B → C occurred more than once.

That is an observation.

---

# Recurring Does Not Mean Causal

Be careful.

From:

```text
A → B → C
```

repeating several times, we cannot automatically conclude:

> A causes B and B causes C.

The history establishes recurrence.

Causal interpretation requires more evidence.

This is Awareness protecting Continuity from overinterpretation.

---

# Practice 8 — Recurring Pattern

Imagine this history:

```text
NORMAL
   ↓
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
   ↓
UNCERTAIN
   ↓
NORMAL
   ↓
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
   ↓
UNCERTAIN
```

Ask:

> What sequence recurred?

> How many parts are in the recurring sequence?

> What observation can you make safely?

> What causal claim would go beyond the available history?

A careful result is:

> This sequence appears repeatedly in the recorded history.

Not:

> We have proven what causes it.

---

# Broken Patterns

Now suppose the familiar sequence becomes:

```text
NORMAL
   ↓
HIGH_TRAFFIC_POSSIBLE
   ↓
DIFFERENT_PATTERN
```

The recurring pattern has broken.

That break may be more interesting than another successful repetition.

Ask:

> Where did the sequence diverge?

> What state was expected?

> What state appeared instead?

> What remained the same before the break?

> What new information might explain the difference?

---

# Practice 9 — Preserve the Break

Write:

```text
Expected sequence:
...

Observed sequence:
...

Shared prefix:
...

Break point:
...

Expected state:
...

Observed state:
...

Possible explanations:
...

Additional evidence needed:
...
```

Notice that:

```text
Possible explanations
```

comes after:

```text
Observed break
```

Detect first.

Explain second.

---

# Patterns Can Become Observations

At first, the system may observe:

```text
request volume
response time
failed logins
```

Later, after preserving history, it may also observe:

```text
recurring state sequence
```

or:

```text
pattern break
```

That is interesting.

The system's history becomes another object available for Awareness.

---

# Apply the Earlier Guides Again

A recurring pattern can be inspected with AACCC.

## Awareness

What sequence actually recurred?

## Alignment

Does the recurrence correspond with another system condition?

## Coherence

Does the proposed explanation fit every occurrence?

## Clarity

Can we show the pattern without overstating what it proves?

## Continuity

What persisted across occurrences, and what changed?

The sequence loops back into the full toolkit.

---

# Provenance

Continuity becomes much stronger when we preserve provenance.

A useful question is not only:

> What changed?

but:

> Why do we believe it changed?

For example:

```text
State₀
  ↓
Observation
  ↓
Decision
  ↓
Update
  ↓
State₁
```

Now the transition has a reason we can inspect later.

---

# Practice 10 — Add Provenance

Take a simple transition:

```text
Draft
  ↓
Revision
  ↓
Final
```

Add:

```text
Reason for revision:
...

Evidence:
...

Who or what introduced the change:
...

What was intentionally preserved:
...

What remained uncertain:
...
```

The history becomes more useful because the transition has context.

# Software Version Example

Imagine:

```text
v1.0
 ↓
v1.1
 ↓
v1.2
 ↓
v2.0
```

A version number tells us:

> Something changed.

It does not tell us exactly what.

For useful Continuity, we may need:

- change history;
- interface changes;
- behavior changes;
- deprecated features;
- migration notes;
- preserved invariants.

The label is a pointer to history.

It is not the history itself.

---

# Practice 11 — What Must Stay Stable?

Imagine an application is being updated.

Choose which of these should probably be preserved or deliberately versioned:

```text
User data

Public API behavior

Known security flaw

Configuration format

Brand color

Documented privacy promise

Internal variable name

Database schema

Audit history
```

Then explain your choice.

Different systems preserve different things.

Continuity requires knowing what matters.

---

# Document Revision Example

Documents have continuity too.

Imagine:

```text
Draft 1
 ↓
Draft 2
 ↓
Draft 3
```

Draft 3 may be clearer.

But ask:

> Did an important qualification disappear?

> Did a speculative claim become stated as fact?

> Did attribution survive?

> Did a citation remain attached to the claim it supports?

> Did the meaning change while the wording improved?

This connects Continuity directly to provenance and Clarity.

---

# Practice 12 — Compare Two Versions

Take two versions of a paragraph.

Mark:

```text
Added:
...

Removed:
...

Reworded:
...

Meaning preserved:
...

Meaning changed:
...

Uncertain:
...
```

Do not start with:

> Which version is better?

Start with:

> What changed?

Then evaluate.

---

# AI and Continuity

An AI assistant can help compare versions.

Try:

> Compare these two versions and separate what changed from what persisted.

Or:

> Identify any claims that became stronger or weaker.

Or:

> What assumptions appear in the new version that were not present earlier?

Or:

> Did the meaning drift even though the wording became clearer?

Then inspect the AI's comparison yourself.

The AI may miss something.

That becomes another Continuity exercise.

---

# AI Conversation Drift

Long AI conversations provide another good example.

You begin with:

```text
Question A
```

After many turns, the conversation may slowly become:

```text
Question B
```

without anyone noticing exactly when.

Ask:

> What was the original goal?

> What is the current goal?

> Which transition changed the direction?

> Was the change intentional?

This is conversational Continuity.

---

# Practice 13 — Conversation Checkpoint

During a long problem-solving session, stop and write:

```text
Original goal:
...

Current goal:
...

What changed:
...

Why it changed:
...

Still relevant:
...

No longer relevant:
...

Next step:
...
```

That simple checkpoint can prevent accidental drift.

# Formal Change

A Lean theorem may compile successfully.

Then you change:

- a definition;
- a type;
- a hypothesis;
- an operation;
- a theorem statement.

Compile again.

Now ask:

> Which proofs survived?

> Which failed?

> Why?

This makes change unusually visible.

---

# A Formal Continuity Record

For a changed formal model, write:

```text
Before:
...

Change:
...

Proofs preserved:
...

Proofs broken:
...

Reason:
...

New assumptions:
...

Removed assumptions:
...

Meaning preserved:
...

Meaning changed:
...
```

Now you're tracking more than compiler success.

You're tracking formal continuity.

---

# Compiler Failure Can Be Evidence

Suppose:

```text
Version 1
compiles
```

and:

```text
Version 2
fails
```

Do not immediately conclude:

> Version 2 is wrong.

Ask:

- What changed?
- Which goal now fails?
- Did the theorem's meaning change?
- Did a dependency change?
- Did an assumption disappear?
- Did the formal environment change?

The difference between versions is evidence.

---

# Same Proof, Different Meaning

There is also a subtler risk.

A theorem may continue to compile after a definition changes.

That sounds reassuring.

But if the definition's meaning changed, the theorem may now represent something different.

So Continuity asks us to inspect semantic change as well as compiler change.

Passing today does not prove:

> Same meaning as yesterday.

# Continuity and the Five Records

By this stage, you already have four earlier tools:

```text
Awareness Record
Alignment Record
Coherence Record
Clarity Record
```

Continuity does not replace them.

It adds history.

A Continuity Record can reference earlier work:

```text
Before:
What did the earlier records establish?

Change:
What happened?

After:
What now appears true?

Persisted:
Which earlier relationships remain?

Drift:
Which shifted gradually?

Break:
Which stopped holding?

Revision:
What needs another look?
```

This is where the Five Guides begin functioning as a combined toolkit.

# The Most Important Continuity Question

After completing the record, ask:

> What was supposed to persist?

This prevents two opposite mistakes.

## Mistake 1

Treating all change as failure.

## Mistake 2

Treating all change as harmless.

The answer depends on what the system was expected to preserve.

---

# Continuity and Safety

Some systems contain relationships that should not casually drift.

For example:

- safety constraints;
- access controls;
- privacy commitments;
- critical interfaces;
- documented assumptions.

If those change, the system should make the change visible.

Continuity helps identify when:

> This was not merely another update.

It changed something important.

---

# Continuity and Learning

Learning itself should contain change.

If your understanding never changes after encountering new evidence, something may be wrong.

A healthy learning history may look like:

```text
Initial idea
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Challenge
    ↓
Revision
    ↓
Better question
```

Continuity preserves enough of that path to understand the revision.

---

# Continuity and Identity

Not every continuity problem has a single correct answer.

Consider:

> At what point is a heavily repaired bicycle no longer the same bicycle?

Or:

> At what point does a revised theory become a new theory?

Or:

> At what point does rewritten software become a different system?

These questions may depend on which properties we choose to preserve.

The useful exercise is identifying those properties.

---

# Continuity and Boundaries

Awareness taught us that boundaries matter.

Continuity depends on them too.

Suppose:

```text
Component A changes.
```

At the component boundary, continuity may be broken.

At the whole-system boundary, continuity may remain.

Or the reverse.

Always ask:

> Continuity of what?

Then:

> Across which boundary?

That makes the question much clearer.

---

# Continuity and Alignment

A relationship may persist across time.

For example:

```text
A₀ ↔ B₀
```

later becomes:

```text
A₁ ↔ B₁
```

The values changed.

But the relationship may remain aligned.

Or perhaps:

```text
A₁ ↔ ?
```

The old mapping no longer works.

Continuity can therefore include the continuity of Alignment itself.

---

# Continuity and Coherence

A system can remain stable while gradually becoming incoherent.

Or it can change dramatically while becoming more coherent.

Continuity and Coherence answer different questions.

Ask:

> What persisted?

and separately:

> Does the current structure hold together?

Do not substitute one for the other.

---

# Continuity and Clarity

A history may exist but still be impossible to understand.

For example:

```text
Changed stuff.
Fixed things.
Updated model.
Final version.
```

Technically, that's a history.

It isn't very useful.

A clear continuity record shows:

```text
what changed
why
what persisted
what broke
what remains uncertain
```

Clarity makes history inspectable.

# Final Practice — One Problem, Five Questions

Choose one fresh changing system.

It might be:

- a software project;
- a repaired object;
- a revised document;
- an evolving belief;
- a research model;
- a recurring problem;
- or something from everyday life.

Now use all five questions.

## Awareness

> What am I observing?

Separate what you know from what you infer.

## Alignment

> What corresponds between the earlier and later states?

Preserve mismatches.

## Coherence

> Does the current explanation of the change hold together?

Locate gaps.

## Clarity

> Can I describe the transition so someone else can inspect it?

Preserve meaning.

## Continuity

> What changed, what persisted, and how did we get here?

Preserve the path.

This is the first time the complete toolkit operates on one problem.
