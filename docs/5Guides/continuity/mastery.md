# Continuity Mastery

> What persists through change?

You have reached the fifth Mastery Challenge.

Continuity brings the earlier Guides together because change gives us more than one thing to inspect.

There is:

```text
What was
   ↓
What happened
   ↓
What is
```

And sometimes:

```text
What happened before
        ↓
What happened again
        ↓
What began to drift
        ↓
What eventually broke
```

Your challenge is not to prevent change.

Your challenge is to make the change inspectable.

---

# The Mastery Challenge

Imagine you are helping maintain a small community website.

The site has one important principle:

> Visitors should be able to read and explore the site without creating an account.

Over time, several changes occur.

## Version 1

The site contains public educational material.

Anyone can:

- visit;
- read;
- follow links;
- leave without identifying themselves.

There are no student accounts.

---

## Version 2

Interactive learning Guides are added.

Learners can work through exercises in their browser.

Progress is temporary.

Closing the browser may lose unfinished progress.

The original no-account reading experience remains available.

---

## Version 3

Badges are added.

A learner can complete a mastery challenge and receive a badge during the session.

The development team begins discussing whether badge progress should persist between visits.

No account requirement has yet been added.

---

## Version 4

Someone proposes:

> Create a required account so learners can save badges and continue where they left off.

The proposal sounds useful.

It would solve a real persistence problem.

But it also changes something important.

---

# Your Task

Create a Continuity Record for the system.

Do not begin by deciding whether Version 4 is good or bad.

First inspect the transition.

---

# Step 1 — Awareness

Ask:

> What is actually present in each version?

Separate observations from conclusions.

For example:

```text
Version 1:
No account is required to read the material.

Version 2:
Interactive Guides are added.
Temporary progress exists.

Version 3:
Session badges are added.
Persistent badges are being discussed.

Version 4:
A required-account proposal is introduced.
```

Now list anything you do not know.

For example:

```text
Unknown:
Whether persistent progress could be implemented without
requiring an account.
```

Do not silently fill that gap.

---

# Step 2 — Alignment

Compare the versions.

Ask:

> What corresponds across all four versions?

Possible relationships to inspect include:

```text
Educational material
        ↕
Educational material

Learner
        ↕
Learner

Guide progress
        ↕
Guide progress

Badge
        ↕
Badge
```

Now identify what does not map cleanly.

For example:

```text
Version 1:
Anonymous access

Version 4 proposal:
Required identity/account state
```

That difference deserves attention.

Do not force it into alignment simply because the newer feature has benefits.

---

# Step 3 — Coherence

Now ask:

> Do the current goals still hold together?

The system wants:

```text
Accessible learning
+
Progress persistence
+
Badges
+
Privacy
```

Can all four coexist?

Maybe.

Maybe not under the proposed implementation.

The mastery task is not to invent the perfect technical solution.

It is to identify where the current proposal introduces tension.

Ask:

- Does required identity follow from the need for persistence?
- Is account creation a requirement or one possible implementation?
- Does the new proposal conflict with an earlier design principle?
- Is important information missing before a decision can be made?

Preserve uncertainty where appropriate.

---

# Step 4 — Clarity

Describe the change clearly.

Avoid:

> Version 4 improves the learning system.

That already evaluates the change.

Avoid:

> Version 4 destroys privacy.

That may also go beyond what has been established.

Prefer something inspectable:

> Version 4 proposes making accounts mandatory in order to provide persistent progress and badges. Earlier versions allowed learners to use the educational material without creating an account. The proposed change therefore introduces a new identity requirement while attempting to solve a persistence problem.

Now another person can inspect the transition without inheriting your conclusion first.

---

# Step 5 — Continuity

Now ask the central question:

> What was supposed to persist?

Possible candidates include:

- access to educational material;
- learner choice;
- the no-required-account principle;
- progress through the Guides;
- badge meaning;
- privacy expectations;
- continuity of the learning experience itself.

Do not assume all of them carry the same importance.

Your job is to identify which relationships matter and explain why.

You do not need to fill every field simply because it exists.

Use the structure to make your reasoning inspectable.

---

# Important Distinction

A new feature can be useful and still create a continuity problem.

Likewise, preserving an old rule can become unhelpful if circumstances change.

So avoid:

```text
Old = good
New = bad
```

and:

```text
New = progress
Old = obsolete
```

Instead ask:

> What function does the change serve?

Then:

> What relationship does the change disturb?

Then:

> Is that relationship still important?

That is a much stronger Continuity analysis.

---

# Look for Drift

Version 4 appears to contain an obvious proposed change.

But there may also be gradual drift across the versions.

Trace the sequence:

```text
Public material
      ↓
Interactive Guides
      ↓
Temporary progress
      ↓
Badges
      ↓
Desire for persistence
      ↓
Proposal for required accounts
```

Ask:

> At which point did identity become relevant?

Then:

> Was identity actually required by the earlier learning goal?

Then:

> Is the proposed requirement a continuation of the original goal, or one particular solution to a later problem?

Do not answer before inspecting the sequence.

---

# What Is the Invariant?

An invariant is something expected to remain true while other things change.

For this exercise, identify at least one candidate invariant.

For example:

```text
A learner can access the educational material
without being required to create an identity.
```

Then ask:

> Did Versions 1 through 3 preserve this?

> Would Version 4 preserve it?

> If not, is changing the invariant intentional?

> Is the reason visible?

An invariant may legitimately change.

If it does, the transition should be explicit.

---

# Feature Continuity vs. Value Continuity

A useful distinction is:

## Feature Continuity

Does the system still provide the same capability?

## Value Continuity

Does the system still preserve the principle behind the capability?

For example:

```text
Feature:
Save progress

Value:
Learner privacy
```

A new implementation might improve one while weakening another.

Continuity helps expose the tradeoff.

---

# A Second Boundary

Now change the observational boundary.

Instead of examining the entire learning system, inspect only:

```text
Badge persistence
```

The account proposal may look very reasonable.

Now expand the boundary to include:

```text
Badge persistence
+
Access
+
Privacy
+
Learner choice
```

Does your judgment change?

This is why Awareness still matters inside Continuity.

The boundary affects what becomes visible.

---

# History Without Judgment

Before recommending anything, try writing the history in neutral form:

```text
Version 1 established anonymous public access.

Version 2 added interactive learning while retaining
the earlier access model.

Version 3 added session-based badges and introduced
a desire for persistence across visits.

Version 4 proposes required accounts as a mechanism
for persistent progress and badges.
```

Now ask:

> Where is the structural transition?

That is a cleaner starting point than jumping directly into advocacy.

---

# What Evidence Would Help?

A strong Continuity analysis should expose what is still missing.

You might want to know:

- whether progress can persist locally;
- whether badges need server-side storage;
- whether optional accounts would satisfy the goal;
- whether learners actually want persistent progress;
- what information an account would require;
- whether anonymous or pseudonymous persistence is possible;
- what security responsibilities persistence introduces.

You do not need to solve those questions here.

Recognizing that they matter is enough.

# Mastery Criteria

You are demonstrating Continuity when you can substantially do the following.

## Establish the Earlier State

Can you describe what existed before the important change?

## Identify Change

Can you identify what actually changed without immediately evaluating it?

## Identify Persistence

Can you identify meaningful structures or relationships that survived?

## Detect Drift

Can you recognize gradual movement away from an earlier state or principle?

## Detect Breaks

Can you identify a transition where an earlier relationship stops holding?

## Preserve History

Can you trace how the current state emerged rather than examining it in isolation?

## Preserve Uncertainty

Can you identify what you do not yet know?

## Identify Important Invariants

Can you explain what may need to remain stable and why?

## Integrate Earlier Guides

Can you use Awareness, Alignment, Coherence, and Clarity while reasoning about change?

# What Mastery Does Not Require

You do not need to:

- find the perfect implementation;
- decide that all change is bad;
- preserve every earlier feature;
- eliminate every uncertainty;
- use formal terminology;
- use Lean;
- use AI;
- reach the same implementation preference as another learner.

The mastery target is structural:

> Can you make the transition inspectable?

# A Useful Retry

If your first analysis becomes mostly opinion, pause.

Remove words such as:

```text
better
worse
obviously
clearly
terrible
good
bad
```

Temporarily.

Describe the transition without them.

Then reintroduce evaluation only after the structure is visible.

That can be surprisingly helpful.
