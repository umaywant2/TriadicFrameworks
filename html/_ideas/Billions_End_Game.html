Billion’s End

A Multiplayer Simulation of Wealth, Power, Stewardship, and System Survival

Core premise

Billion’s End is a persistent multiplayer world combining the social life of The Sims, the shared-world exploration of a large online role-playing game, and the economic competition of Monopoly.

Players build homes, businesses, technologies, institutions, communities, and alliances. Every player begins with limited resources and attempts to reach a net worth of one billion credits.

Reaching one billion is not the final victory.

It is the beginning of the real game.

Players who exceed the wealth ceiling experience progressively larger reversals:

  • First crossing: lose 1 million credits
  • Second crossing: lose 10 million credits
  • Third crossing: lose 100 million credits
  • Fourth crossing: lose 1 billion credits and leave the conventional wealth competition

Because productive capacity increases during play, staying below the ceiling becomes progressively harder. Players must learn to circulate wealth before accumulated success removes them from the economic game.

The central challenge is therefore not:

How much can you accumulate?

It is:

How much value can you create without allowing ownership to consume your place in the world?

The reversal

Most economic games reward uninterrupted accumulation. Billion’s End reverses that structure.

The strongest player is not the player who permanently possesses the most wealth. The strongest player is the one who can:

  • create value repeatedly;
  • anticipate systemic consequences;
  • distribute resources intelligently;
  • build durable institutions;
  • preserve individual agency;
  • strengthen communities without controlling them;
  • and remain productive without converting success into domination.

Giving is not presented as a sentimental side activity. It is a strategic survival mechanism.

The living world

The game takes place in a shared world containing cities, rural regions, natural resources, universities, transportation networks, media systems, governments, cooperatives, markets, and community institutions.

Players may become:

  • entrepreneurs;
  • builders;
  • researchers;
  • artists;
  • educators;
  • investors;
  • public servants;
  • environmental stewards;
  • inventors;
  • organizers;
  • journalists;
  • or institutional founders.

No single profession guarantees success. Each role depends on other parts of the world.

A technology company may require public infrastructure. A city may require private innovation. A university may generate discoveries but depend on community trust. A profitable industry may create environmental or social costs that later destabilize the entire region.

The game therefore models wealth as part of a living system rather than as an isolated score.

Wealth and value

Players track several forms of capital:

  1. Financial capital
    Currency, property, investments, and business ownership.

  2. Social capital
    Trust, relationships, alliances, and community legitimacy.

  3. Knowledge capital
    Skills, research, education, discoveries, and open knowledge.

  4. Civic capital
    Public institutions, participatory systems, rights protections, and shared infrastructure.

  5. Ecological capital
    Land health, energy resilience, biodiversity, water, and long-term resource stability.

A player may be financially wealthy while becoming socially isolated, ecologically destructive, or institutionally dangerous.

The world reacts to the complete pattern.

Crossing the billion-credit threshold

Whenever a player exceeds one billion credits, the game creates a Billion Event.

During a Billion Event, the player receives a warning and a limited period in which to redirect excess wealth through legitimate actions.

Available responses may include:

  • raising wages;
  • sharing ownership with workers;
  • funding independent research;
  • building affordable housing;
  • supporting schools or health systems;
  • restoring damaged environments;
  • releasing technology into an open commons;
  • financing community-owned infrastructure;
  • creating grants for other players;
  • reducing debt burdens;
  • establishing independently governed public trusts;
  • or investing in regions neglected by the dominant economy.

The player cannot simply transfer money to a controlled subsidiary, temporary ally, or private foundation that the player still dominates. The system evaluates whether ownership and decision-making power actually moved.

Progressive pressure

Each round increases productive efficiency.

New technologies, automation, trade networks, and accumulated knowledge make it easier to generate wealth. This means that even players who successfully remain below one billion will eventually approach the threshold again.

The pressure is intentional.

Players must repeatedly redesign their relationship with wealth. A strategy that worked during the first Billion Event may fail during the second or third.

The game asks whether a successful person can continue creating without continually concentrating control.

The fourth crossing

At the fourth Billion Event, the player loses one billion credits and exits the conventional accumulation contest.

However, this is not necessarily game over.

The player enters one of several Legacy Paths:

  • Founder: builds an institution that must survive without personal control.
  • Mentor: helps newer players develop skills and avoid previous mistakes.
  • Trustee: manages common resources under strict public accountability.
  • Explorer: enters new regions where financial wealth has limited usefulness.
  • Citizen Auditor: examines economic activity and challenges corruption.
  • World Steward: supports ecological and civic systems without accumulating private ownership.

The transition asks a final question:

Can a player remain meaningful after wealth is no longer the primary source of power?

Player victory

The game has no single universal winner.

At the end of a season, several forms of achievement are recognized:

  • greatest sustainable value created;
  • strongest community resilience;
  • most knowledge released into the commons;
  • highest worker and citizen participation;
  • most successful ecological restoration;
  • most trustworthy institution;
  • best recovery from systemic failure;
  • and longest productive participation without coercive control.

A player may become extremely wealthy and still receive a poor final assessment. Another player may possess relatively little while helping build a world that remains prosperous, free, and resilient.

The final score measures both individual achievement and the condition of the world left behind.

AI participants

AI agents inhabit the world alongside human players.

They may serve as:

  • citizens;
  • workers;
  • customers;
  • competitors;
  • advisers;
  • journalists;
  • researchers;
  • negotiators;
  • auditors;
  • or simulation observers.

AI agents have defined goals, limited knowledge, recorded decision histories, and inspectable sources. They do not operate as invisible omniscient judges.

Students may examine:

  • what information an agent used;
  • which assumptions shaped its recommendation;
  • whether multiple agents share the same training lineage;
  • whether apparent agreement represents independent judgment;
  • and how the agent’s objective affected its behavior.

This makes the world a laboratory for both economics and AI literacy.

The Judges

The game is supervised by a plural oversight system rather than a single central authority.

Human council

A rotating group of students, educators, community representatives, and subject specialists interprets difficult cases.

AI observers

Auditable agents detect patterns such as hidden ownership, coordinated manipulation, market capture, ecological damage, and circular transfers.

Citizen review

Players may challenge an assessment, inspect the evidence, and request a public review.

World metrics

The system monitors:

  • wealth concentration;
  • freedom and mobility;
  • access to education;
  • housing stability;
  • ecological conditions;
  • institutional trust;
  • coercive control;
  • innovation;
  • health of local economies;
  • and the number of players able to participate meaningfully.

No judge has permanent or unquestionable authority.

Judging decisions retain their evidence, reasoning, dissenting opinions, and revision history.

Student learning goals

Students use the game to explore:

  • compounding and exponential growth;
  • wealth concentration;
  • incentives and unintended consequences;
  • taxation and redistribution;
  • ownership versus stewardship;
  • public goods;
  • individual freedom;
  • institutional design;
  • cooperation and competition;
  • environmental externalities;
  • algorithmic governance;
  • AI provenance and lineage;
  • ethical disagreement;
  • and the difference between winning a game and sustaining a world.

Students are not told that one economic system is automatically correct. They are asked to form hypotheses, play under different rules, observe consequences, and defend conclusions with evidence.

Classroom investigation

Each class may run several versions of the same world:

  • no wealth ceiling;
  • a fixed billion-credit ceiling;
  • progressive Billion Events;
  • voluntary giving only;
  • taxation and redistribution;
  • worker ownership;
  • universal basic services;
  • strong inheritance;
  • limited inheritance;
  • AI-governed allocation;
  • citizen-governed allocation;
  • and mixed human-AI governance.

Students compare the worlds and ask:

  1. Which rules produced innovation?
  2. Which rules preserved individual freedom?
  3. Which systems concentrated power?
  4. Did giving create dependency or capacity?
  5. Could players manipulate the judges?
  6. Did AI agents reproduce the assumptions of their training?
  7. Which apparent successes merely moved costs elsewhere?
  8. What happened to people who refused to participate?
  9. Which institutions survived their founders?
  10. What kind of world remained after the richest players left?

Design principle

Billion’s End is not a punishment fantasy about wealthy people.

It is a simulation of a deeper structural problem:

What happens when the ability to create value grows faster than the ability to release control?

The game does not ask students to hate success.

It asks them to explore whether success can mature into stewardship before accumulation becomes exclusion.

Tagline

Build. Share. Belong.

Wealth is a tool, not a trophy.
