Resonance Recall Test

A cue may precede renewed access to a pattern, but renewed access alone does not tell us whether the pattern was retrieved, reconstructed, relearned, regenerated, or newly synthesized.

The Resonance Recall Test is an exploratory protocol for examining whether a cue, representation, or comparison is associated with renewed access to previously encountered material.

The basic experimental structure is:

EARLIER EXPOSURE
      ↓
ACCESS INTERVAL
      ↓
BASELINE TEST
      ↓
CUE / TRIGGER
      ↓
RESONANCE REPORT
      ↓
RECOGNITION / RECALL TEST
      ↓
RECONSTRUCTION TEST
      ↓
DELAYED RETEST
      ↓
TRANSFER TEST

The experiment does not assume that:

successful renewed access
        =
intact stored pattern

It compares several candidate explanations.

Research Question

The primary question is:

Does exposure to a structurally related cue change an observer's ability to recognize, reconstruct, recall, or apply a previously encountered relationship?

Secondary questions include:

Does the cue improve recognition?

Does it improve unaided recall?

Does it improve reconstruction?

Does it improve functional use?

Does it increase false recognition?

Does the effect persist after delay?

Does the relationship transfer
to a new representation or problem?

The test separates these outcomes rather than treating them as one phenomenon.

Core Hypothesis

A bounded working hypothesis is:

A structurally related cue
may increase access to
a previously encountered
relationship.

The hypothesis does not specify the mechanism.

Possible mechanisms include:

ordinary associative memory

context reinstatement

cue-dependent retrieval

reconstruction

rapid relearning

implicit learning

pattern completion

new synthesis

expectation

or another process

Null Possibilities

The test should preserve several null or limiting outcomes.

The cue produces no measurable
change in access.

The cue increases familiarity
without improving accuracy.

The cue increases confidence
without improving performance.

The cue produces false recognition.

The cue improves performance only
while the cue remains present.

A nonrelated control cue produces
the same improvement.

Any of these outcomes would narrow the Re-Ignition interpretation.

What the Test Is Measuring

The test should distinguish among several variables.

FAMILIARITY

Does the material feel known?

RECOGNITION

Can the observer identify
the previously encountered pattern?

RECALL

Can the observer reproduce
relevant information without
the original material present?

RECONSTRUCTION

Can the observer rebuild
the relationship from partial cues?

FUNCTION

Can the observer use
the relationship successfully?

TRANSFER

Can the observer identify or apply
the relationship in a new form?

These outcomes should not be collapsed into a single score unless a later protocol justifies doing so.

Recognition Is Not Recall

The test must preserve:

RECOGNITION
    ≠
RECALL

An observer may recognize the correct structure when shown several options while being unable to produce it independently.

For example:

Recognition:

"I know which model is correct."

versus:

Recall:

"I can reproduce the model
without seeing the options."

Both are useful measurements.

They represent different access conditions.

Recall Is Not Reconstruction

Likewise:

RECALL
    ≠
RECONSTRUCTION

Recall attempts to reproduce previously encountered material.

Reconstruction may combine:

partial trace

current knowledge

cue information

reasoning

and new representation

to produce a related structure.

The resulting answer may be useful without being an exact reproduction.

Reconstruction Is Not Failure

A reconstructed relationship should not automatically be classified as failed recall.

Suppose the earlier form was:

A → B → C

The later observer produces:

A → X → C

Possible interpretations include:

partial continuity

reconstruction

model improvement

error

or new synthesis

Comparison with the earlier form is required.

Resonance Is Not Accuracy

A cue may produce a strong experience of correspondence.

cue
  ↓
resonance
  ↓
high familiarity

But:

strong resonance
      ≠
accurate recall

The protocol should record subjective resonance separately from objective or task-specific performance.

Confidence Is Not Accuracy

Students or participants may report:

high confidence

while producing:

low accuracy

or:

low confidence

while producing:

high accuracy

Therefore:

CONFIDENCE
    ≠
ACCURACY

Confidence should be recorded as an observer-state variable rather than treated as verification.

Trigger Is Not Cause

The cue used in the experiment functions initially as a candidate Resonance Trigger.

CUE
 ↓
RENEWED ACCESS

The test may support:

association

or:

systematic contribution

It does not automatically establish the complete causal mechanism.

The permanent guardrail is:

TRIGGER
    ≠
PROVEN CAUSE

Experimental Units

The protocol may be performed with:

individual students

small groups

repeated trials

multiple representations

multiple cue types

or independent observers

The smallest unit is:

one participant

one target relationship

one baseline condition

one cue condition

one comparison

Larger studies require additional controls and statistical planning.

Target Pattern

The target should be a relationship rather than merely an isolated fact where possible.

Examples include:

ordering rule

transformation

structural analogy

problem-solving method

diagram relationship

conceptual dependency

pattern sequence

operator relationship

A relationship supports richer comparison among recognition, recall, reconstruction, and transfer.

Target Selection

A useful target should be:

clear enough to score

complex enough to contain
a meaningful relationship

representable in more than one form

capable of producing
plausible alternatives

Avoid targets so vague that every response can be interpreted as a match.

Earlier Exposure

The participant must have some earlier relationship to the target if the test is intended to examine renewed access.

Evidence of earlier exposure may include:

documented instruction

completed exercise

saved response

prior performance

recorded demonstration

or verified interaction

Without evidence of earlier exposure, the experiment may instead be measuring first-time learning or discovery.

Earlier Access

Exposure alone does not establish successful earlier access.

The protocol should distinguish:

EXPOSURE

The participant encountered
the material.

from:

EARLIER ACCESS

The participant demonstrated
some usable understanding
or performance.

Where practical, preserve an earlier artifact for later comparison.

Earlier Artifact

An earlier artifact might include:

written explanation

diagram

solution

recorded performance

classification

prediction

or completed task

This artifact provides a comparison point.

EARLIER ARTIFACT
        ↓
LATER RESPONSE
        ↓
INVARIANT / DIFFERENCE ANALYSIS

Without an earlier artifact, continuity claims become weaker.

Dormant or Reduced-Access Condition

Before cue exposure, conduct a baseline test.

The baseline asks whether the target is currently:

accessible

weakly accessible

cue-dependent

partially accessible

or unavailable

Do not label the pattern dormant solely because time passed.

Dormancy requires evidence of reduced access.

Baseline Test

The baseline should occur before the candidate cue.

Possible baseline prompts include:

Describe the relationship.

Reproduce the method.

Solve a related problem.

Draw the structure.

Identify the rule.

Predict the next state.

The prompt should avoid revealing the target relationship unnecessarily.

Baseline Contamination

A baseline prompt can itself become a trigger.

This creates an important design problem.

BASELINE TEST
      ↓
may activate
the target relationship

To reduce contamination:

use minimal prompts

record first response quickly

avoid corrective feedback

separate recognition items
from recall items

The baseline can never be assumed completely neutral.

Its possible trigger role should be documented.

Cue Conditions

The protocol may compare several cue types.

RELATED CUE

Contains structure meaningfully related to the target.

SURFACE-SIMILAR CUE

Looks similar but does not preserve the critical relationship.

UNRELATED CUE

Does not intentionally share the target structure.

NO-CUE CONDITION

Provides another attempt without an added cue.

These conditions help distinguish structural cue effects from repetition or surface familiarity.

Related Cue

A Related Cue should preserve a defined aspect of the target.

For example:

same transformation

same ordering

same dependency

same operator

same relationship
in another domain

The shared relationship should be specified before the test.

Do not define it only after seeing the response.

Surface-Similar Cue

A Surface-Similar Cue may share:

appearance

vocabulary

color

layout

or topic

without sharing the target relationship.

This helps test whether recognition depends on:

structural correspondence

or:

surface familiarity

Unrelated Cue

An Unrelated Cue should be comparable in general complexity or presentation while lacking the intended target relationship.

Its purpose is not to be meaningless.

It provides a comparison for general attention or task repetition.

No-Cue Condition

The No-Cue condition asks the participant to attempt the task again without a new cue.

This can help estimate the effect of:

repetition

additional time

or continued effort

separately from cue-specific effects.

Cue Equivalence

Cue conditions should be kept as comparable as practical.

Potential differences include:

length

difficulty

novelty

emotional salience

visual complexity

familiarity

and required effort

If these differ substantially, they become alternative explanations.

Suggested Experimental Sequence

A minimal individual protocol is:

1. Confirm Earlier Exposure

2. Preserve Earlier Artifact

3. Conduct Baseline Test

4. Record Confidence

5. Present Cue

6. Record Immediate Response

7. Record Resonance / Familiarity

8. Conduct Recognition Test

9. Conduct Recall Test

10. Conduct Reconstruction Test

11. Conduct Transfer Test

12. Delay

13. Conduct Delayed Retest

14. Compare With Baseline
    and Earlier Artifact

15. Identify Invariants

16. Identify Differences

17. Compare Explanations

Do not provide corrective feedback until the relevant testing stages are complete.

Phase 1: Earlier-State Verification

Record:

Was the participant
previously exposed?

Did the participant previously
demonstrate access?

What artifact survives?

What was the earlier
performance level?

If these cannot be established:

earlier access
    =
UNKNOWN

The test may still examine cue-assisted learning, but the Re-Ignition claim should be weakened.

Phase 2: Baseline Access

Before the cue, record:

free recall

recognition

functional performance

representation

confidence

response time

where appropriate.

The baseline establishes the current access state.

Phase 3: Cue Exposure

Present one cue condition.

Record:

cue type

cue content

cue duration

observer response

first noticeable change

The cue should be documented precisely enough to repeat.

Phase 4: Immediate Resonance Report

Immediately after cue exposure, ask for a brief report before explanation expands.

Prompts may include:

Did anything seem familiar?

Did any relationship become noticeable?

What appeared first?

Do not explain why yet.

The purpose is to preserve the earliest signal.

Phase 5: Recognition Test

Present several candidate patterns or relationships.

Ask the participant to identify the previously encountered target.

Record:

selection

confidence

response time

reasons given

Include plausible distractors where appropriate.

Phase 6: Free Recall Test

Remove the options.

Ask the participant to reproduce or describe the target independently.

Record:

correct elements

missing elements

added elements

ordering

relationship

confidence

Recognition and recall results should remain separate.

Phase 7: Reconstruction Test

Provide incomplete elements and ask the participant to rebuild the relationship.

For example:

A → ? → C

or:

Given these components,
reconstruct the process.

Record:

preserved relations

new components

errors

alternative valid forms

strategy

Phase 8: Functional Test

Ask the participant to use the relationship.

Possible tasks include:

solve a related problem

predict an outcome

classify an example

explain a transformation

complete a sequence

apply the method

Functional performance may provide stronger evidence of usable access than familiarity alone.

Phase 9: Transfer Test

Present the same relationship in a different form or domain.

Target in Form A
      ↓
Transfer Item in Form B

Ask whether the participant can identify or apply the invariant relationship.

Transfer helps test whether access depends on:

one specific representation

or:

a more general relationship

Phase 10: Delayed Retest

After a defined interval, repeat selected measures.

Record:

recognition

free recall

functional use

transfer

confidence

The interval should be reported exactly.

Do not describe an interval as short or long without defining it in the protocol.

Why Delay Matters

Immediate performance may reflect:

temporary activation

working memory

cue persistence

task repetition

Delayed access provides another comparison.

But delayed success still does not identify the persistence mechanism automatically.

Phase 11: Comparison

Compare:

EARLIER ARTIFACT

BASELINE RESPONSE

POST-CUE RESPONSE

DELAYED RESPONSE

TRANSFER RESPONSE

Then locate:

invariants

changed components

newly added components

lost components

errors

confidence changes

Invariant Analysis

Ask what survives across the responses.

Earlier Artifact ──┐
                   │
Baseline ──────────┼── Invariant R?
                   │
Post-Cue ──────────┤
                   │
Delayed ───────────┘

Possible invariants include:

ordering

operator

functional relationship

transformation

error signature

structural dependency

representation feature

An invariant may support continuity without proving unchanged storage.

Difference Analysis

Also record what changes.

EARLIER
│
├── A
├── B
└── C

LATER
│
├── A
├── B
└── D

Then ask:

Was C lost?

Was D reconstructed?

Was D newly learned?

Is D an improvement?

Is the difference error?

The answer may remain unresolved.

Candidate Outcome Patterns

Several result patterns may occur.

Pattern A: Recognition Without Recall

Baseline:
low access

Post-Cue Recognition:
improved

Free Recall:
little improvement

Possible interpretations include:

cue-dependent recognition

familiarity

weak retrieval

insufficient reconstruction

or another process

Pattern B: Recall Improvement

Baseline Recall:
low

Post-Cue Recall:
higher

This supports:

cue-associated renewed access

It does not identify whether the mechanism is retrieval, reconstruction, or relearning.

Pattern C: Functional Recovery

Baseline Function:
poor

Post-Cue Function:
improved

This suggests renewed usable access.

Compare error patterns with earlier performance.

Pattern D: Reconstruction Without Exact Recall

Exact Recall:
low

Functional Reconstruction:
successful

This may indicate:

retained generating rule

partial structural continuity

reasoning-based reconstruction

or new synthesis

Further comparison is required.

Pattern E: Familiarity Inflation

Familiarity:
high

Accuracy:
unchanged or lower

This weakens the interpretation that subjective resonance alone reflects reliable access.

Pattern F: Confidence Inflation

Confidence:
higher

Performance:
unchanged

The cue may change observer confidence without improving the target relationship.

Pattern G: Cue-Specific Access

With Cue:
access improves

Without Cue:
access declines

This suggests continued cue dependence.

It may indicate incomplete stabilization.

Pattern H: Delayed Persistence

Post-Cue:
improved

Delayed:
improvement persists

This supports stabilization more strongly than immediate performance alone.

It does not determine mechanism.

Pattern I: No Cue Advantage

Related Cue
    ≈
Unrelated Cue
    ≈
No-Cue Repetition

This weakens the claim that structural resonance in the related cue produced the improvement.

Pattern J: Transfer

Target Form:
recognized

New Form:
relationship also identified

This supports the possibility of a representation-general relationship.

Transfer should still be compared with relevant controls.

Observer-State Capture

The participant's state may affect results.

Record relevant variables where appropriate.

attention

fatigue

prior knowledge

expectation

motivation

current context

representation preference

confidence

previous exposure

Only record variables ethically and relevantly.

Do not infer hidden personal characteristics from performance.

Expectation Effects

If participants know which cue is expected to help, expectation may influence:

attention

effort

confidence

reported familiarity

Where practical, avoid revealing the preferred hypothesis before testing is complete.

At minimum, document what the participant was told.

Demand Characteristics

Students may try to produce the answer they believe the instructor expects.

This is especially likely if they know the experiment is called:

Resonance Recall Test

A classroom facilitator may use a neutral activity title during administration.

The formal name can be explained during debriefing.

Scoring Recognition

Recognition scoring may include:

correct identification

false identification

confidence

response time

explanation quality

The exact scoring criteria should be defined before responses are reviewed.

Scoring Recall

Recall scoring may include:

correct components

correct ordering

correct relationships

omissions

intrusions

transformations

functional equivalence

Exact reproduction and functional equivalence should be scored separately where relevant.

Scoring Reconstruction

Reconstruction scoring may distinguish:

preserved invariant

valid new representation

incorrect addition

missing relation

successful function

unsupported narrative

A reconstruction can differ in form while preserving the target relationship.

Scoring Transfer

Transfer may be evaluated through:

relationship identification

successful application

prediction

explanation

new-form reconstruction

The transfer task should not be so similar that it merely repeats the original representation.

Nor should it be so different that failure becomes uninterpretable.

Subjective Resonance Scale

If a subjective resonance rating is used, define it explicitly.

Example:

0
No familiarity or correspondence

1
Weak familiarity

2
Possible relationship noticed

3
Relationship seems identifiable

4
Strong recognition

5
Immediate and confident recognition

This is a subjective reporting scale.

It is not a direct measure of structural truth.

Observer Report

A brief observer report can ask:

What did you notice first?

What seemed familiar?

What relationship appeared?

When did you feel certain?

What explanation came afterward?

The report should distinguish:

first signal

from:

later interpretation

False-Positive Analysis

The test should include opportunities to identify false recognition.

Possible methods include:

plausible distractors

surface-similar alternatives

novel patterns

incorrect structural matches

Record:

which distractors were selected

confidence

reasoning

shared surface features

False positives may reveal what the cue actually activated.

False-Negative Analysis

A participant may possess usable structural knowledge while failing explicit recognition.

For example:

verbal recognition:
low

functional performance:
high

This result should not be forced into a simple failure category.

It may indicate:

implicit access

representation mismatch

language difficulty

or another process

These remain hypotheses.

Representation Effects

The same target may be presented as:

text

diagram

sequence

table

analogy

problem

Different forms may produce different access.

This allows the test to examine:

Does the cue restore access
to the relationship?

Or only to one representation
of the relationship?

Cross-Form Test

A strong cross-form design uses:

Earlier Exposure:
Form A

Cue:
Form B

Test:
Form C

If the participant identifies the same invariant across all three, the result is harder to explain through simple visual repetition alone.

It still does not establish the complete mechanism.

Cross-Context Test

The target relationship may also be tested in a new context.

Learning Context A
        ↓
Testing Context B

If performance remains stable:

context-general access

becomes more plausible.

If performance fails:

context dependence

may need to be represented explicitly.

Cross-Observer Test

Multiple participants can receive comparable conditions.

Observer A → Response A

Observer B → Response B

Observer C → Response C

Compare:

shared recognition

shared invariant

false positives

cue dependence

transfer

confidence

Agreement may strengthen reproducibility.

Shared instruction and shared expectations must still be considered.

Within-Observer Test

A participant may experience multiple cue conditions.

However, earlier trials may change later observer states.

Trial 1
   ↓
learning / recognition
   ↓
Trial 2 observer
is no longer identical

Therefore:

trial order

may affect results.

Record or vary the order where appropriate.

Order Effects

A cue presented first may alter all later tests.

Possible strategies include:

different condition orders

separate participant groups

different target patterns

or explicit order documentation

The appropriate design depends on available participants and goals.

Repetition Effects

Performance may improve simply because the participant attempts the task repeatedly.

The No-Cue condition helps compare:

cue effect

with:

practice / repetition effect

Do not attribute all post-cue improvement to resonance if repeated effort provides an adequate explanation.

Learning During the Test

The experiment itself may teach the target.

baseline
   ↓
cue
   ↓
test

can become:

learning sequence

This is not necessarily a flaw.

But the interpretation should distinguish:

renewed earlier access

from:

new learning during the test

Debriefing

After data collection, explain:

the purpose of the cue conditions

the difference between
recognition and recall

the role of alternative explanations

the reason false positives matter

Students should be invited to revise their interpretation.

The debrief is part of the educational value.

Ethical Boundary

The protocol should avoid presenting ordinary memory variation as evidence of:

hidden memories

suppressed memories

alternate timelines

external influence

or abnormal mental states

The test examines cue-associated access to learned relationships.

It is not a clinical memory-recovery procedure.

No High-Stakes Memory Claims

Do not use this exploratory protocol to validate:

eyewitness testimony

traumatic-memory claims

medical diagnosis

legal claims

or accusations

Those domains require specialized professional standards and validated procedures.

The present protocol is intended for educational and low-stakes research settings.

Data Separation

Keep the following data categories distinct.

SUBJECTIVE REPORT

What the participant felt
or recognized.

TASK PERFORMANCE

What the participant produced
or successfully applied.

OBSERVER INTERPRETATION

What the facilitator believes
the result means.

MECHANISM HYPOTHESIS

What process might explain
the result.

These are related.

They are not interchangeable.

Re-Ignition Stage Mapping

The test can map results onto the Ignition branch.

DORMANT PATTERN

Baseline shows reduced access.

RESONANCE TRIGGER

Cue precedes changed access.

RESONANCE

Participant reports or demonstrates
candidate correspondence.

RECOGNITION EVENT

Relationship becomes explicitly
identifiable.

REINTEGRATION

Participant connects the relationship
to current understanding.

STABILIZATION

Relationship survives delayed,
cross-form, or transfer testing.

A stage should be marked:

UNKNOWN

when evidence is insufficient.

Dormancy Evidence

The experiment should not claim dormancy unless there is evidence of:

earlier access

and:

current reduced access

Without both:

dormant pattern
    =
not established

The test may still reveal learning or cue effects.

Trigger Evidence

A cue becomes a supported trigger candidate when:

it precedes changed access

and preferably:

related cue conditions
outperform relevant controls

Even then:

trigger
    ≠
complete cause

Recognition Evidence

Recognition is supported when the participant can explicitly identify the target relationship.

Do not infer recognition entirely from:

confidence

emotion

or familiarity

Use task evidence where possible.

Reintegration Evidence

Reintegration may be supported when the participant can:

explain the relationship

connect it with current knowledge

revise an earlier model

or use it coherently

This may still be provisional.

Stabilization Evidence

Stabilization may be supported when the participant can revisit the relationship through:

delayed retest

cross-form representation

new context

independent application

or repeated functional use

The specific criterion should be defined before the test.

Discriminators

Potential discriminators include:

related cue versus
surface-similar cue

related cue versus
unrelated cue

cue versus no-cue repetition

recognition versus free recall

exact recall versus
functional reconstruction

immediate versus delayed performance

No single discriminator necessarily resolves every mechanism.

Minimum Supported Claim

A successful result may support only:

"Under the tested conditions,
the related cue was associated
with improved access to the
target relationship."

This does not yet establish:

the target was stored intact

resonance was the physical mechanism

the observer accessed
another timeline

or the relationship existed
outside representation

The smaller claim is still useful.

Stronger Claim Requirements

To support a stronger continuity claim, the experiment would need evidence such as:

documented earlier access

reduced baseline access

post-cue improvement

shared structural invariants

limited surface overlap

delayed persistence

functional transfer

and weaker competing explanations

Even this would not automatically establish the complete storage mechanism.

Claim Ladder

Level 1: Earlier Exposure

The participant previously
encountered the target.

Level 2: Earlier Access

The participant previously
demonstrated access to the target.

Level 3: Reduced Baseline Access

Current unaided access
was lower or incomplete.

Level 4: Cue Association

Access changed after cue exposure.

Level 5: Cue Specificity

The related cue outperformed
relevant comparison conditions.

Level 6: Structural Continuity

Defined invariants connect
earlier and later performance.

Level 7: Stabilization

The relationship survived
delay, transfer, or repeated testing.

Level 8: Mechanistic Model

A defined process explains
the observed access change.

Level 9: General Mechanism

The mechanism generalizes
across relevant participants,
targets, and conditions.

Level 10: Ontological Claim

The mechanism reflects
the underlying nature
of memory, time, or reality.

The exploratory test operates primarily at the earlier levels.

Falsification Boundary

A Resonance Recall hypothesis should be weakened when:

the related cue does not
outperform comparison cues

improvement is explained
by repetition alone

confidence increases
without performance

false recognition increases
without accurate access

no invariant connects
earlier and later responses

the effect disappears

Common Interpretation Errors

Cue-Cause Collapse

Cue preceded improvement
        ↓
Cue caused improvement

Not yet established.

Recognition-Recall Collapse

Participant recognized target
        ↓
Participant recalled target

These are different outcomes.

Familiarity-Accuracy Collapse

Participant felt familiarity
        ↓
Participant was correct

Not necessarily.

Continuity-Identity Collapse

Earlier and later forms
share invariants
        ↓
They are identical

Continuity does not require perfect identity.

Stabilization-Truth Collapse

        ↓
Interpretation is true

Persistence alone does not establish mechanism or ontology.

Representation-Ontology Collapse

Participant represented
the result as another timeline
        ↓
Another physical timeline exists

The representation and ontology remain separate.

The Resonance Recall Test begins with:

EARLIER EXPOSURE
      ↓
EVIDENCE OF EARLIER ACCESS
      ↓
REDUCED BASELINE ACCESS
      ↓
CANDIDATE CUE

      ↓
RECOGNITION
      ↓
RECALL
      ↓

      ↓

The test separates:

reconstruction

function

transfer

stabilization

because improvement in one does not automatically imply improvement in all.

Its central investigative workflow is:

        ↓

REVISE

The permanent guardrails are:

CUE
    ≠
PROVEN CAUSE

FAMILIARITY
    ≠
ACCURACY

RECOGNITION
    ≠
RECALL

RECALL
    ≠
RECONSTRUCTION

CONTINUITY
    ≠

STABILIZATION
    ≠
TRUTH

The guiding discipline is:

Verify earlier access before claiming dormancy.
Measure baseline access before presenting the cue.
Separate familiarity from accuracy.
Separate recognition from recall and reconstruction.
Compare related cues with plausible controls.
Search for invariants across earlier, immediate, delayed, and transfer responses.
Let the complete result pattern determine how much continuity the evidence supports.

In its smallest form:

CUE
 ↓
ACCESS CHANGES?
 ↓
WHAT CHANGED?
 ↓
WHAT SURVIVED?

A positive result does not tell us that a complete pattern waited unchanged to be retrieved.

It gives us a measured transition from which competing persistence and access models can finally be compared.

Main writing choice: I separated familiarity, recognition, free recall, reconstruction, function, transfer, and delayed stability into distinct outcomes. That prevents a strong “I know this” feeling from carrying the evidential weight of accurate recall or usable skill, while still preserving the subjective resonance report as legitimate data.
