Absolutely. I think the five should feel like one learning architecture repeated through five progressively different lenses, rather than five unrelated courses. The first, Awareness, becomes the reference implementation.

Five-Workflow Student Architecture

0. Common architecture

Every workflow follows the same recognizable learning rhythm: Orient → Explore → Check → Apply → Demonstrate → Reflect → Continue

The student therefore learns how to learn through TriadicFrameworks during Awareness. After that, navigation and mechanics become familiar and attention can move toward the concepts themselves.

Shared components

Stage Purpose AI role1
1. Orientation Explain what the student is about to investigate and why Answer questions, establish context
2. Guided Exploration Walk through selected material, examples, and observations Explain, reframe, provide alternate examples
3. Awareness Check Optional first-pass assessment of what was retained Help identify gaps without simply supplying answers
4. Applied Exercise Use the idea on something concrete Coach reasoning and ask productive questions
5. Mastery Checkpoint Demonstrate practical understanding Support preparation and reflection
6. Badge Mark completion of the workflow Explain what the achievement represents
7. Reflection Student considers what changed in their understanding Help articulate observations if requested
8. Voluntary Footprint Optional minimal feedback + optional country None required
9. Continue Introduce the next workflow Provide orientation to the next stage

A crucial distinction: Awareness Check ≠ Mastery Checkpoint.

The first asks:

What stuck the first time through?

The second asks:

Can you actually use what you've learned?

That prevents the system from degenerating into five read-and-quiz modules.

Workflow 1: Awareness

Core question

What am I observing? This is our introductory workflow and establishes the entire learning pattern.

Student journey

Orientation Introduce observation, perspective, assumptions, boundaries, and the difference between encountering information and noticing structure within it.

Guided Exploration Use approachable examples before asking the learner to engage more deeply with the framework.

Optional Awareness Check Ask what they noticed and retained during their first encounter. No penalty. No “failure.” Its purpose is itself an exercise in awareness.

Applied Exercise Present something unfamiliar enough that the learner must deliberately observe rather than merely repeat terminology.

They identify things such as: - what is present, - what they believe they are observing, - assumptions they have introduced, - uncertainty, - missing information, - distinctions that might matter.

Mastery Checkpoint The learner demonstrates that they can consciously inspect an observation rather than simply accept their initial interpretation of it.

Award

Awareness Badge Meaning:

Completed the Awareness workflow and demonstrated practical use of its core concepts.

Not certification. Not professional accreditation. A learning milestone.

Optional footprint

After the badge: Where are you learning from? [ Country ▾ ]

One thought about your experience:

Both optional. Then: Continue to Alignment →

Workflow 2: Alignment

Core question

Do the things I'm comparing actually correspond? Now that the learner is deliberately observing, we introduce relationships, mappings, definitions, frames of reference, and mismatches.

Guided Exploration

Students encounter situations where things appear to agree but may operate under different assumptions or meanings.

Awareness Check

Can the student recognize obvious examples of alignment and misalignment after initial exposure?

Applied Exercise

Give them two descriptions, models, observations, claims, or representations and ask them to identify: - shared elements, - differing assumptions, - mismatched terminology, - boundary differences, - compatible mappings, - unresolved conflicts.

The AI can be particularly useful here because the student can challenge a proposed mapping:

“Why do you think those two things align?”

That turns the AI into a reasoning partner rather than an answer machine.

Mastery Checkpoint

The learner demonstrates an ability to establish or reject an alignment and explain why.

Award

Alignment Badge Then voluntary reflection, country, and: Continue to Coherence →

Workflow 3: Coherence

Core question

Does this hold together? Alignment establishes relationships. Coherence examines whether the emerging structure remains internally workable.

Guided Exploration

Introduce examples containing: - compatible pieces, - contradictions, - missing connections, - unsupported transitions, - internally consistent structures.

Awareness Check

Can the learner spot obvious coherence problems on first inspection?

Applied Exercise

Give them a small structured argument, model, explanation, or system. Ask:

What fits? What conflicts? What is missing? What requires an unstated assumption?

Then allow the learner to modify the structure and examine whether coherence improves.

Mastery Checkpoint

The learner demonstrates that they can identify a coherence problem and meaningfully improve or explain it.

Award

Coherence Badge Reflection → optional footprint → next workflow.

Workflow 4: Clarity

Core question

Can this be understood well enough to inspect and use?

This one is interesting because by now the learner already possesses three tools. They're aware of what they're observing. They can examine alignment. They can inspect coherence. Now we ask whether the resulting representation is sufficiently clear.

Guided Exploration

Compare things that may be: - correct but difficult to understand, - internally coherent but poorly expressed, - ambiguous, - underspecified, - overloaded with terminology, - apparently simple but misleading.

The point is not merely better writing. It's structural clarity.

Applied Exercise

Give the learner an unclear representation and ask them to improve it without quietly changing its meaning. That's a very important challenge. The AI can offer alternative representations, while the learner evaluates whether those alternatives preserve the original structure.

Mastery Checkpoint

The learner demonstrates the ability to recognize ambiguity or structural obscurity and improve clarity while preserving meaning.

Award

Clarity Badge Reflection → optional footprint → final workflow.

Workflow 5: Continuity

Core question

** Does it still hold when something changes?**

This becomes the culmination rather naturally.

The learner has developed: Awareness of the system. Alignment between its relevant elements. Coherence within its structure. Clarity sufficient to inspect that structure.

Now the system moves.

Guided Exploration

Something changes between states. - A condition changes. - Evidence arrives. - An assumption disappears. - A boundary moves. - A representation is revised.

The question becomes:

What persists, what changes, and what breaks?

Applied Exercise

Give the learner an initial state and a changed state.

Have them trace: - preserved structure, - altered relationships, - lost assumptions, - newly introduced information, - emerging contradictions, - whether conclusions still follow. For the first time, all five ideas can explicitly operate together.

Mastery Checkpoint

Rather than testing Continuity in isolation, this could become the integrative exercise.

The learner needs to demonstrate:

Awareness → Alignment → Coherence → Clarity → Continuity

on one manageable problem.

Award

Continuity Badge And with it:

Something closer to:

You now have five structural lenses you can apply independently. Use them on other problems, question their limits, and test whether they remain useful outside the examples presented here.

Then offer the final optional reflection.

Awareness Student Workflow — Detailed Lesson Plan

Five-Guide Sequence: Awareness → Alignment → Coherence → Clarity → Continuity
Guide: 1 of 5
Core Question: What am I observing?
Mode: Student-led, optionally AI-assisted
Assessment Model: Awareness first, application second, mastery last
Privacy Model: No tracking required; any learner feedback is voluntary
Exit: Awareness Badge → optional reflection → Alignment

1. Purpose

The Awareness workflow introduces the student to the first discipline of the five-guide sequence:

Before interpreting, aligning, correcting, or explaining a system, become aware of what is actually being observed.

The goal is not to persuade the student to adopt a particular interpretation.

The goal is to help the student distinguish among:

  • what is directly presented,
  • what they notice,
  • what they infer,
  • what they assume,
  • what remains uncertain,
  • what may be missing,
  • and what changes when the observation is reconsidered.

Awareness therefore serves two purposes.

First, it introduces a useful structural practice in its own right.

Second, it establishes the learning pattern that will be reused throughout the remaining four guides.

2. Learning Principle

The workflow begins with a deliberately simple question:

What am I observing?

That question should initially remain open.

The student should not immediately be given a complicated formal vocabulary for answering it.

Instead, the workflow should let the student encounter the difficulty naturally.

A student sees something.

The student forms an interpretation.

The student is then encouraged to inspect the distance between:

Observation

and

Interpretation

That distance is where the Awareness lesson begins.

3. Learning Objectives

By the end of the workflow, the student should be able to:

  1. describe an observation before attempting to explain it;
  2. distinguish observation from interpretation;
  3. identify assumptions introduced by the observer;
  4. recognize uncertainty rather than automatically resolving it;
  5. identify potentially missing information;
  6. reconsider an initial interpretation when new information appears;
  7. explain how observer perspective can affect a description;
  8. apply the Awareness process to an unfamiliar example;
  9. use AI as an optional reasoning partner without treating the AI as the authority;
  10. recognize when additional observation is required before proceeding to stronger claims.

The student does not need to demonstrate that there is one uniquely correct interpretation.

The student needs to demonstrate that they can inspect their own process of observing and interpreting.

4. Workflow Overview

The Awareness workflow follows the common five-guide architecture:

  1. Encounter
  2. Observe
  3. Explore
  4. Awareness Check
  5. Revisit
  6. Apply
  7. Demonstrate
  8. Reflect
  9. Badge
  10. Optional Footprint
  11. Continue

The workflow should feel like an investigation rather than an examination.

5. Stage One — Encounter

Student Prompt

Present the student with a simple object, situation, statement, image, short scenario, or system.

Ask only:

What do you notice?

Do not initially ask:

What does this mean?

Do not immediately introduce RTT terminology.

Do not tell the student what they are expected to notice.

The purpose of the first encounter is to establish an authentic initial observation.

Student Action

The student records a short description.

There should be no scoring at this stage.

Design Principle

The student's original response should remain available later.

It becomes useful evidence when the student returns to the same material after completing the guided exploration.

6. Stage Two — Separate Observation from Interpretation

The student now examines their first response.

Introduce two questions:

What did the material actually give you?

and:

What did you add while interpreting it?

The distinction does not need to be absolute.

That ambiguity is educationally useful.

The student begins categorizing parts of their response.

Possible categories:

  • directly observed,
  • inferred,
  • assumed,
  • uncertain,
  • unknown.

For example:

Statement Student Classification
I can directly identify this Observed
I think this caused that Inferred
I expected this behavior Assumed
I cannot determine this yet Uncertain
The information isn't provided Unknown

The exercise is not about perfect classification.

It is about making the student aware that these categories exist.

7. Stage Three — Guided Exploration

The student is now introduced to several lenses for inspecting an observation.

Lens A — Boundary

Ask:

What are you treating as part of the thing being observed?

Then:

What are you leaving outside it?

The student should begin noticing that observation usually implies some boundary, even when that boundary has not been explicitly stated.

Lens B — Perspective

Ask:

From what position are you observing this?

Then:

Could another observer reasonably describe it differently?

The objective is not relativism.

Different observations should not automatically be treated as equally supported.

The point is simply that perspective can affect what becomes visible, relevant, or emphasized.

Lens C — Assumption

Ask:

What did you need to assume in order to reach your interpretation?

If the student replies that they made no assumptions, encourage another inspection.

Possible prompts:

  • Did you assume a cause?
  • Did you assume an intention?
  • Did you assume a category?
  • Did you assume that something remained unchanged?
  • Did you assume information that was not explicitly available?

Lens D — Uncertainty

Ask:

What don't you know yet?

Students should be encouraged to preserve uncertainty where the observation does not support a conclusion.

"I don't know yet" is a legitimate structural result.

Lens E — Missing Information

Ask:

What additional observation would help you distinguish among the possibilities?

This moves the student away from passive uncertainty and toward disciplined investigation.

8. Stage Four — Optional Awareness Check

The Awareness Check occurs before the main applied exercise.

Its purpose is diagnostic rather than punitive.

Introduce it explicitly:

This is not a graded test.

This short check helps you see what stayed with you after your first exploration. If something did not stick, that is useful information too.

The check should be short.

Possible question types include:

Recognition

Which statement is an observation and which includes an interpretation?

Assumption Detection

Which conclusion requires information that has not been provided?

Uncertainty

Is there enough information to make this claim?

Boundary Recognition

What appears to have been included or excluded from the observation?

Reflection

What is one thing you are less certain about now than when you started?

That final question is particularly important.

A productive Awareness workflow may temporarily make the learner less certain.

That is not necessarily regression.

It may indicate that previously invisible assumptions have become visible.

9. Awareness Check Result

Do not present a traditional score as the primary result.

Prefer a structural summary such as:

Your First Awareness Snapshot

You readily noticed:

  • observation versus interpretation,
  • explicit information.

You may want to revisit:

  • hidden assumptions,
  • observation boundaries.

Still developing:

  • preserving uncertainty before reaching a conclusion.

Then offer:

Revisit a concept

or

Continue to the exercise

The learner controls the next step.

10. Stage Five — Revisit

The student now returns briefly to the original encounter.

Show the original response.

Ask:

Would you describe this differently now?

Then:

What changed?

The student may:

  • leave the response unchanged,
  • revise it,
  • qualify it,
  • identify an assumption,
  • add uncertainty,
  • identify missing information.

An unchanged answer is not automatically wrong.

The important question is whether the student can now explain why they retained or changed it.

This comparison makes learning visible without requiring surveillance or analytics.

11. Stage Six — Applied Exercise

Now provide a new example.

The new example should not simply reproduce the introductory exercise with different nouns.

It should require the student to transfer the Awareness process.

Student Task

Analyze the unfamiliar example using five prompts:

1. Observe

What is directly available to you?

2. Bound

What are you treating as inside or outside the observation?

3. Separate

What parts of your description are observations, interpretations, or assumptions?

4. Preserve

What remains uncertain or unknown?

5. Extend

What additional information would you seek before making a stronger conclusion?

The student produces a small Awareness Record.

12. Awareness Record

The Awareness Record can become the first reusable student artifact in the five-guide series.

Suggested structure:

Observation

What I can currently observe:

Boundary

What I am treating as part of the observation:

Interpretation

What I think the observation may mean:

Assumptions

What I may be adding:

Uncertainty

What I cannot determine yet:

Missing Information

What I would like to observe next:

Revision

What changed after I reconsidered the observation:

This structure should remain lightweight.

It is a thinking aid, not bureaucratic paperwork.

13. Stage Seven — AI-Assisted Path

AI assistance is available throughout the workflow but is never required.

The AI's role is:

Help the student inspect their reasoning, not replace it.

Good AI prompts include:

Ask me questions that help separate my observations from my interpretations.

What assumptions might I be making?

Give me another possible interpretation, but do not tell me which interpretation is correct.

What information would distinguish between these possibilities?

Challenge my observation without replacing it with your own answer.

Help me identify what I still don't know.

The AI may also generate additional practice examples.

14. AI Constraint

The AI should not become the hidden answer key.

If the student asks:

"What's the correct answer?"

the preferred educational response is not simply to withhold information.

Instead, the AI should help determine what kind of answer is possible from the available evidence.

For example:

Let's separate what the example establishes from what would require an additional assumption.

This preserves the student's agency while still providing genuine assistance.

15. AI Fallibility as Part of the Lesson

Awareness provides an unusually good opportunity to establish an important principle for all five guides:

AI output is also something to observe.

The student may therefore occasionally be invited to examine an AI response using the same Awareness process.

Ask:

  • What did the AI actually claim?
  • What evidence did it use?
  • What assumptions does the response appear to introduce?
  • What remains uncertain?
  • What should be independently checked?

The AI is therefore not positioned outside the framework.

Its output becomes another object available for inspection.

16. Stage Eight — Mastery Challenge

The mastery challenge should use a fresh example.

The student should not succeed merely by memorizing the earlier exercises.

Challenge

Provide an unfamiliar situation containing some combination of:

  • direct observations,
  • plausible interpretations,
  • hidden assumptions,
  • missing context,
  • uncertainty.

Ask the learner to create an Awareness Record without being walked through every step.

17. Mastery Criteria

Mastery should be demonstrated when the learner can substantially do the following:

Observation

Identify what is actually available in the example.

Interpretation

Separate at least the major interpretations from direct observation.

Assumption

Recognize significant assumptions introduced by the observer.

Uncertainty

Explicitly preserve an important unresolved question.

Boundary

Recognize that the selected observational boundary affects the analysis.

Investigation

Identify useful additional information or observation.

Revision

Demonstrate willingness to revise an interpretation when warranted.

The objective is not perfect terminology.

The objective is demonstrated use of the Awareness process.

18. Mastery Is Retryable

A learner who does not yet demonstrate mastery has not "failed Awareness."

The system should instead identify the area that would benefit from another pass.

For example:

Almost there.

You separated observation from interpretation clearly, but one of your conclusions still depends on information the example does not provide.

Revisit Assumption and Uncertainty, then try another example.

The learner may retry with a new example.

This makes mastery developmental rather than punitive.

19. Stage Nine — Awareness Badge

When the mastery criteria are satisfied:

Awareness Badge

Awareness

Completed the first Five Guides student workflow and demonstrated practical use of Awareness.

The badge should represent a specific achievement.

It should not imply:

  • professional certification,
  • accreditation,
  • scientific endorsement,
  • expertise in RTT,
  • mastery of the entire TriadicFrameworks corpus.

It represents completion of this learning milestone.

20. Badge Metadata

A lightweight badge artifact could eventually contain:

  • badge name,
  • guide identifier,
  • workflow version,
  • completion criteria version,
  • completion state.

Personal identity does not need to be inherent in the badge.

That design question can be addressed during implementation planning.

For now the governing principle is:

Award the learning artifact without requiring the learner to surrender unnecessary personal information.

21. Stage Ten — Reflection

Immediately after mastery, pause before sending the learner onward.

Ask one or two questions.

For example:

What changed about the way you looked at the problem?

and:

What will you pay attention to the next time you encounter something unfamiliar?

This reflection belongs primarily to the learner.

It does not automatically need to be submitted anywhere.

24. Privacy Principle

The workflow should preserve a distinction that is central to the project:

Observation is not permission to observe the learner.

A framework teaching Awareness should be especially careful about that distinction.

The student is learning to inspect systems.

The system does not therefore gain an entitlement to inspect the student.

Any footprint should be:

  • optional,
  • deliberate,
  • minimal,
  • understandable,
  • and separable from mastery.

Declining to leave feedback must change nothing about the student's achievement.

25. Stage Eleven — Transition to Alignment

Awareness ends by opening the next question naturally.

The student has learned to ask:

What am I observing?

Now introduce a complication.

Suppose there are two observations.

Or two descriptions.

Or two models.

Or two observers.

They appear to refer to the same thing.

But do they?

That creates the doorway to Guide Two:

Alignment

What corresponds?

The learner now has something important they did not have before.

They know not to start by forcing the two representations together.

First observe.

Then determine whether and how they align.

1. Purpose

The Alignment workflow begins where Awareness ends.

In Awareness, the student learned to ask:

What am I observing?

They practiced separating:

  • observation from interpretation,
  • evidence from assumption,
  • known from uncertain,
  • and the chosen observational boundary from the larger situation.

Alignment introduces the next structural problem:

When two things appear related, what actually corresponds between them?

The learner may encounter:

  • two observations,
  • two descriptions,
  • two models,
  • two representations,
  • two systems,
  • two moments in time,
  • or two observers describing apparently similar phenomena.

The temptation is to decide immediately that they are either:

the same

or

different.

Alignment asks the student to slow down.

Before deciding whether two things agree, disagree, match, conflict, or represent the same structure, the learner must determine what is actually being compared.

The purpose of this workflow is therefore not to produce agreement.

It is to teach the learner how to inspect correspondence.

2. Learning Principle

The central question is deliberately simple:

What corresponds?

The student should gradually discover that correspondence can occur at different levels.

Two things may:

  • use different words while describing similar structures,
  • use the same words while referring to different structures,
  • share some components but not others,
  • correspond under one boundary but not another,
  • align at one level while diverging at another,
  • or appear aligned only because an unstated assumption connects them.

Alignment therefore should not initially be reduced to:

Does A equal B?

Instead, the student learns to ask:

In what way does A correspond to B, and what evidence supports that mapping?

3. Relationship to RTT

Within the current RTT material, Alignment is treated structurally rather than morally or evaluatively.

RTT describes alignment across three broad dimensions:

  • structural alignment,
  • temporal alignment,
  • resonance or pattern-level alignment.

The current RTT material also treats alignment as regime-dependent rather than static and associates it with boundaries, operators, dimensional access, stability, drift, transitions, and pattern behavior.

For the student workflow, however, these formal structures should not be front-loaded.

The student should first experience the problem of correspondence.

Formal RTT terminology can then provide one possible structured vocabulary for examining what the learner has already encountered.

This preserves the pattern established during Awareness:

Experience the structural problem before receiving the formal machinery used to describe it.

4. Learning Objectives

By the end of the Alignment workflow, the student should be able to:

  1. identify the things being compared;
  2. describe the basis on which a proposed correspondence is being made;
  3. distinguish similarity from actual correspondence;
  4. recognize partial alignment;
  5. identify important mismatches;
  6. recognize when identical terminology hides different meanings;
  7. recognize when different terminology may describe corresponding structures;
  8. identify assumptions required to establish a mapping;
  9. preserve unresolved differences rather than forcing alignment;
  10. explain why a proposed alignment is supported, unsupported, or incomplete;
  11. revise a mapping when boundaries or evidence change;
  12. use AI as an optional partner for testing mappings rather than accepting AI-generated mappings as authoritative.

The learner does not need to make everything align.

A legitimate conclusion may be:

These elements do not currently align.

or:

There is not enough information to establish alignment.

Recognizing non-alignment is part of Alignment mastery.

6. Stage One — Encounter

Present two simple representations.

They might be:

  • two descriptions of the same event,
  • two diagrams of similar systems,
  • two short explanations,
  • two classifications,
  • two observations,
  • two models,
  • or two versions of something over time.

Ask:

Do these things correspond?

Then:

What makes you think so?

Do not initially provide categories of alignment.

Do not tell the student whether the pair was designed to align.

The learner should make an initial judgment.

Student Action

The student records:

Initial judgment

  • strongly aligned,
  • partly aligned,
  • probably not aligned,
  • uncertain.

Then:

Reason

What led you to that judgment?

There is no scoring at this stage.

The student's initial mapping should remain available for later comparison.

7. Stage Two — Return to Awareness

Before teaching anything new, briefly invoke the previous workflow.

Ask:

What exactly are you observing in A?

Then:

What exactly are you observing in B?

Then:

What assumptions are you making before comparing them?

This establishes an important dependency:

Alignment depends on Awareness.

Poorly observed entities cannot be reliably aligned.

The student should experience this connection rather than merely being told it exists.

8. Stage Three — Identify the Mapping

Now introduce a simple mapping structure.

Given:

A

and:

B

ask:

What in A do you believe corresponds to what in B?

The student creates explicit pairs.

For example:

A Proposed Correspondence B
A1 ↔ B1
A2 ↔ B2
A3 ? B3
A4 none identified —
— none identified B4

Immediately the learner can see something that the words "these are similar" tend to hide.

Some elements may correspond.

Some may not.

Some may remain uncertain.

Alignment is becoming inspectable.

9. Stage Four — Guided Exploration

The learner is introduced to several lenses for evaluating a proposed mapping.

Lens A — Identity

Ask:

Are these actually the same thing, or merely similar things?

This prevents correspondence from silently becoming identity.

The student should encounter examples where:

A resembles B

does not imply:

A is B

Lens B — Meaning

Ask:

Do the corresponding terms mean the same thing here?

Two systems may use the same word differently.

For example:

state

might refer to:

  • system condition,
  • geographic jurisdiction,
  • stored computational configuration,
  • or another domain-specific concept.

Shared vocabulary alone therefore cannot establish alignment.

The learner should inspect meaning in context.

10. Lens C — Structure

Ask:

Even if the labels differ, do the elements play corresponding structural roles?

This introduces an important inverse case.

Two descriptions may use different terminology while representing similar relationships.

For example:

Input → transformation → output

might structurally correspond to:

Observation → operation → result

without claiming that the terms are universally interchangeable.

The mapping exists because of the relationship being examined, not because the labels happen to match.

11. Lens D — Boundary

Return to a concept learned during Awareness.

Ask:

Are A and B being compared within equivalent boundaries?

A comparison can appear misleading if one representation contains:

  • an entire system,

while the other describes:

  • only one subsystem.

The learner should recognize:

Misaligned boundaries can create apparent misalignment elsewhere.

Before declaring structural disagreement, inspect what each representation includes.

12. Lens E — Scale

Ask:

Are these being observed at comparable scales?

One representation may describe:

  • an individual event,

while another describes:

  • an aggregate pattern.

One may describe:

  • a component,

while another describes:

  • a system.

A correspondence may still exist, but it must not be assumed.

The learner should identify when scale affects the comparison.

13. Lens F — Time

Ask:

Are these observations from comparable states or moments?

Two observations may represent the same system under different conditions.

What initially appears to be structural misalignment may instead reflect change.

Conversely, superficial similarity at two moments does not prove continuity.

This provides the student's first small bridge toward the later Continuity workflow without attempting to teach Continuity yet.

14. Lens G — Function

Ask:

Do these elements perform corresponding roles?

Two components may look different but perform similar functions.

Two components may look similar but perform fundamentally different functions.

The student should therefore consider:

appearance

versus:

role.

15. Lens H — Constraint

Ask:

Are A and B operating under comparable constraints?

A behavior may only make sense within:

  • a particular environment,
  • a rule set,
  • a boundary condition,
  • an available resource,
  • or another limiting context.

Alignment can become misleading when constraints are ignored.

16. Lens I — Assumption

Ask:

What must you assume for this mapping to work?

This explicitly reconnects Alignment to Awareness.

The learner records assumptions rather than allowing them to disappear inside the mapping.

A useful alignment statement therefore starts to look like:

A1 appears to correspond to B1 if assumption X holds.

That is structurally stronger than:

A1 = B1.

17. Lens J — Mismatch

Finally ask:

What doesn't fit?

This question should be given equal status with:

What corresponds?

Students should not interpret mismatch as failure.

A mismatch may be the most informative result of the exercise.

Teach the student to preserve it.

18. The Alignment Spectrum

At this point, introduce several possible outcomes.

A proposed correspondence may be:

Strong

The relevant structures correspond closely within the declared boundary.

Partial

Some important elements correspond while others do not.

Conditional

The correspondence holds only under stated assumptions or conditions.

Uncertain

Available evidence is insufficient to determine whether correspondence holds.

Misaligned

Important structural differences prevent the proposed correspondence.

Unmapped

No useful correspondence has yet been identified.

These should not initially require numerical scores.

The learner is building structural judgment, not optimizing a metric.

19. Stage Five — Optional Alignment Check

The Alignment Check evaluates first-pass understanding.

Again, clearly state:

This is not a graded test.

It is a quick way to see what you already recognize and what may deserve another look.

Keep it short.

Possible exercises follow.

Same Words, Different Meaning

Present two uses of the same term.

Ask:

Does identical terminology establish alignment?

The student should recognize that meaning and role still need inspection.

Different Words, Similar Structure

Present two differently named structures.

Ask:

Is there a plausible structural correspondence?

Require a short explanation.

Partial Mapping

Provide four elements in A and four in B.

Three have reasonable mappings.

One does not.

Ask:

What should happen to the unmatched element?

A good answer preserves the mismatch rather than forcing a pair.

Boundary Problem

Give:

A = entire system

and:

B = one subsystem

Ask:

What should be clarified before comparing them?

Assumption Detection

Provide a proposed mapping that only works if an unstated assumption is accepted.

Ask:

What additional assumption is required?

Uncertainty

Provide insufficient information.

Ask:

Can alignment be established yet?

The correct structural response may simply be:

Not yet.

The student is learning how to align, not learning how to avoid wrong-answer messages.

Maybe fixing a mistake isn't considered an "important change."

Maybe the group values a good outcome more than following the agreed process.

their stated rule and their actual behavior don't fully fit together.

Don't Fix It Yet

This is where Coherence gets interesting.

  • Is information missing?
  • Is there an unstated exception?
  • Has the group's rule changed?
  • Are their words and actions using different standards?
  • Could both decisions make sense under different conditions?

Notice what we're not doing:

We're not forcing the story to become coherent.

We're locating the place where the structure needs more inspection.

Sometimes you'll find a contradiction.

That's a much more interesting kind of Coherence problem.

The goal isn't simply to catch things that are wrong.

The goal is to understand what has to be true for the whole thing to hold together.

How Systems Can Use Triads 🔺

So far, we've mostly practiced these ideas as human skills.

But a system can use a similar approach.

Instead of trying to understand everything at once, it can examine a situation through a small set of related signals:

Three views → one structural check

We'll call that a triad.

The important idea isn't that three magically solves every problem.

It's that three related observations can give a system enough structure to compare relationships instead of trusting a single signal.

1. Awareness — What State Am I In?

Imagine a system monitoring a bicycle.

It might observe:

Triad Observation
Tire pressure Low
Wheel rotation Normal
Rider effort Increasing

One signal tells us something.

Three related signals give us something more interesting.

The system can now form an awareness state:

Something may be changing in the bicycle-rider system.

It hasn't diagnosed the problem yet.

That's important.

Awareness comes first.

2. Alignment — Do the Signals Agree? 🔗

Now compare the signals.

Low tire pressure
↕
Increasing rider effort

Those observations appear related.

But:

Wheel rotation remains normal.

So the system shouldn't simply declare:

Flat tire!

Instead, it can record:

Possible alignment between pressure loss and increased effort.

Now imagine another sensor reports normal tire pressure.

We have a mismatch.

That's useful too.

The system has detected something that needs another look.

3. Coherence — Does the Explanation Hold Together? 🧩

Next the system considers the observations together.

Suppose it proposes:

The tire is losing pressure, creating more rolling resistance and requiring greater rider effort.

Now test that explanation against the triad.

Does it fit:

Pressure?
Yes.

Rotation?
Possibly.

Effort?
Yes.

The explanation may be coherent enough to continue investigating.

But if rider effort suddenly returned to normal while pressure continued falling, the system should reconsider.

Coherence isn't permanent. It can change as new observations arrive.

4. Clarity — Can the Result Be Explained? 🔎

A system could produce:

"Abnormal multi-variable state detected with correlated pressure-effort divergence."

Technically impressive.

Not especially helpful. 😉

A clearer result might be:

The rear tire may be losing pressure.

Reason: Tire pressure decreased while rider effort increased.

Uncertain: Wheel behavior remains normal.

Next check: Confirm the pressure reading before deciding that the tire is the cause.

Now the reasoning is visible.

A human can inspect it.

That's Clarity.

The Pattern

A system can therefore move through the same questions you've been learning:

Awareness

Alignment

Which observations seem to correspond?

Coherence

Do those relationships hold together?

Clarity

Can I express the result so someone else can inspect it?

Or more compactly:

Observe → Compare → Test → Explain

Why Use Triads?

One signal can be misleading.

Two signals give us a comparison.

A third can help test that comparison.

For example:

A ↔ B

looks convincing.

But introduce C:

A ↔ B ↔ C

and suddenly we can ask:

Does C support the relationship?

Does C challenge it?

Or is C telling us we're looking at the problem the wrong way?

That's where triads become useful as a structural learning tool.

Code Example — A Tiny Structural Monitor 💻

Let's turn the login-service example into code.

The goal is not to make the software magically diagnose the problem.

Instead, we'll give it a simple sequence:

Awareness → Alignment → Coherence → Clarity

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Signals:
    login_requests: str
    response_time: str
    failed_logins: str


def awareness(signals: Signals):
    """Record what was actually observed."""
    return {
        "requests": signals.login_requests,
        "response_time": signals.response_time,
        "failures": signals.failed_logins,
    }


def alignment(observed):
    """Look for relationships among observations."""
    return {
        "response_and_failures":
            observed["response_time"] == "increasing"
            and observed["failures"] == "increasing",

        "traffic_explanation":
            observed["requests"] == "increasing",
    }


def coherence(observed, aligned):
    """Test whether a possible explanation fits the observations."""

    if (
        aligned["response_and_failures"]
        and observed["requests"] == "normal"
    ):
        return {
            "state": "plausible",
            "explanation":
                "Login performance may be degrading independently of request volume.",
        }

    return {
        "state": "uncertain",
        "explanation":
            "The current observations do not yet support a clear explanation.",
    }


def clarity(observed, coherent):
    """Turn the structural result into something inspectable."""

    return {
        "status": coherent["state"],
        "observed": observed,
        "explanation": coherent["explanation"],
        "next_step":
            "Gather another observation before identifying a cause.",
    }


signals = Signals(
    login_requests="normal",
    response_time="increasing",
    failed_logins="increasing",
)

observed = awareness(signals)
aligned = alignment(observed)
coherent = coherence(observed, aligned)
report = clarity(observed, coherent)

print(report)

The interesting part isn't really the Python. It's the separation of responsibilities.

1. Awareness

The system records:

What did I observe?

It doesn't diagnose anything yet.

2. Alignment

The system notices:

Response time and failures are both increasing.

But it also preserves:

Request volume is normal.

That third observation helps challenge an easy explanation.

3. Coherence

Now the software tests a tentative explanation against the observations.

It can say:

This explanation is plausible.

without pretending:

This is definitely the cause.

4. Clarity

Finally, the system exposes: - what it observed; - what it currently thinks; - how certain that reasoning is; - and what should be observed next.

That's much more inspectable than:

ERROR: LOGIN SERVICE BAD

😉

The Bigger Idea

Notice what we did not write:

if failures_are_high:
    cause = "login_service"

That would jump almost directly from:

Observation → Conclusion Instead, we created intermediate places where reasoning can be inspected: Observe → Compare → Test → Explain

Or in our Five Guides language:

Awareness → Alignment → Coherence → Clarity

And because each stage produces something we can inspect, another person, another program, or even an AI assistant can ask:

Where did this conclusion come from?

That's when a small example starts becoming something more interesting than an alert. It becomes an inspectable reasoning process.

Implementing the Missing States 🧪

Our first version treated anything outside the expected pattern as simply:

uncertain

Let's make the reasoning more explicit.

We want the system to distinguish between:

  • plausible — the observations support our current explanation;
  • high_traffic_possible — rising traffic provides another plausible explanation;
  • different_pattern — failures are rising without the expected slowdown;
  • insufficient_data — one or more observations are unknown;
  • uncertain — we have observations, but the current model doesn't explain them.

Updated Coherence Function

def coherence(observed, aligned):
    """Test whether our explanations fit the available observations."""

    # Awareness first:
    # Don't reason past information we don't actually have.
    if "unknown" in observed.values():
        return {
            "state": "insufficient_data",
            "explanation":
                "One or more observations are unknown.",
        }

    # Response time and failures are increasing,
    # while request volume remains normal.
    if (
        aligned["response_and_failures"]
        and observed["requests"] == "normal"
    ):
        return {
            "state": "plausible",
            "explanation":
                "Login performance may be degrading independently "
                "of request volume.",
        }

    # All three signals are increasing.
    # Increased traffic now becomes another possible explanation.
    if (
        aligned["response_and_failures"]
        and aligned["traffic_explanation"]
    ):
        return {
            "state": "high_traffic_possible",
            "explanation":
                "Response time, failures, and request volume are all "
                "increasing. Increased traffic may be contributing.",
        }

    # Failures are increasing without the expected response-time change.
    if (
        observed["failures"] == "increasing"
        and observed["response_time"] == "normal"
    ):
        return {
            "state": "different_pattern",
            "explanation":
                "Failures are increasing without a matching increase "
                "in response time.",
        }

    # We have observations, but they don't currently fit
    # one of our modeled patterns.
    return {
        "state": "uncertain",
        "explanation":
            "The observations do not yet support one of the current "
            "explanations.",
    }

def validate_transition(previous_state, new_state):
    """Check whether a state transition is allowed by the model."""

    allowed = ALLOWED_TRANSITIONS.get(previous_state, set())

    if new_state in allowed:
        return {
            "valid": True,
            "from": previous_state,
            "to": new_state,
            "reason": "Transition is allowed by the current model.",
        }

    return {
        "valid": False,
        "from": previous_state,
        "to": new_state,
        "reason":
            "Transition is not currently allowed and should be inspected.",
    }

Create a Transition Record

from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import datetime, timezone


@dataclass
class StateTransition:
    timestamp: datetime
    previous_state: SystemState
    new_state: SystemState
    valid: bool
    reason: str

Each record answers:

  • When did the transition occur?
  • Where did we start?
  • Where did we go?
  • Did the transition fit our model?
  • Why was it accepted or flagged?

Add a History Tracker

class StateHistory:
    def __init__(self, initial_state):
        self.current_state = initial_state
        self.transitions = []

    def transition_to(self, new_state):
        result = validate_transition(
            self.current_state,
            new_state,
        )

        transition = StateTransition(
            timestamp=datetime.now(timezone.utc),
            previous_state=self.current_state,
            new_state=new_state,
            valid=result["valid"],
            reason=result["reason"],
        )

        self.transitions.append(transition)

        if result["valid"]:
            self.current_state = new_state

        return transition

Notice an important design decision:

An unexpected transition is recorded even when it isn't accepted as the new current state.

We don't erase the observation simply because it doesn't fit our model.

from collections import Counter


def find_recurring_patterns(history, pattern_length=3):
    """Find repeated sequences of accepted states."""

    states = []

    if history.transitions:
        states.append(history.transitions[0].previous_state)

    for transition in history.transitions:
        if transition.valid:
            states.append(transition.new_state)

    patterns = [
        tuple(states[i:i + pattern_length])
        for i in range(len(states) - pattern_length + 1)
    ]

    counts = Counter(patterns)

    return {
        pattern: count
        for pattern, count in counts.items()
        if count > 1
    }

def detect_pattern_break(expected, observed):
    """Find where an observed sequence diverges from a known pattern."""

    for position, (expected_state, observed_state) in enumerate(
        zip(expected, observed)
    ):
        if expected_state != observed_state:
            return PatternBreak(
                expected=expected,
                observed=observed,
                break_position=position,
                expected_state=expected_state,
                observed_state=observed_state,
            )

    return None

A broken pattern might mean:

  • the system changed;
  • the surrounding conditions changed;
  • an observation is missing;
  • the recurring pattern was weaker than we thought;
  • our model needs revision;
  • or something genuinely new happened.

We don't know which one yet.

And that's exactly the point.

Detect first. Explain second.

Lean Examples for the AACCC Guides

Yes, this is possible, and I think it could become one of the strongest mathematical threads running through the five guides.

Lean is especially suitable because it separates:

  • definitions,
  • observations and hypotheses,
  • claims,
  • proof steps,
  • and verified conclusions.

A theorem that compiles but expresses the wrong claim is still the wrong theorem. That fits our emphasis on inspectable reasoning rather nicely. 【1-bb9694】【2-5a4109】

Recommended Approach

Use one small mathematical system across all five guides rather than five unrelated examples.

A simple choice would be:

Natural numbers, equality, and a small transformation such as doubling.

For example:

double n = n + n

Each guide would examine a different part of the same proof environment.

1. Awareness

Question

What is actually given, and what must be proved?

The student identifies:

  • the variables;
  • their types;
  • the hypothesis;
  • the target theorem;
  • and anything they may be assuming without evidence.

Lean idea

Given natural numbers a and b, and a hypothesis that a = b, observe the goal:

double a = b + b

The student does not prove it immediately.

First, they inspect the proof state:

  • a b : Nat
  • h : a = b
  • target: double a = b + b

Lesson

The proof state is the observation. The proof strategy is an interpretation of that observation.

2. Alignment

Question

What corresponds?

The student maps:

  • a to b through the hypothesis h;
  • double a to a + a;
  • and, after substitution, a + a to b + b.

Lean idea

The equality hypothesis provides the alignment:

h : a = b

Rewriting with h establishes the correspondence between expressions containing a and expressions containing b.

Lesson

Alignment does not mean that two expressions merely look similar. It means that a declared or proven relationship permits one to correspond with the other.

3. Coherence

Question

Do the definitions, hypotheses, and conclusion hold together?

The student checks whether the proposed proof actually follows from:

  • the definition of double;
  • the equality hypothesis;
  • and the rules of equality.

A deliberately broken theorem could also be introduced:

double a = b + b + 1

The student asks whether the extra + 1 is supported by anything in the proof state.

Lesson

Every part of the conclusion must be supported by the declared structure.

Lean's refusal to close the goal becomes useful evidence that something does not yet hold together.

4. Clarity

Question

Can the theorem and proof be expressed so another person can inspect them?

The student compares:

  • a compressed proof;
  • a step-by-step proof;
  • and a poorly named or ambiguous theorem.

The theorem statement should clearly expose:

  • inputs;
  • hypotheses;
  • transformation;
  • and conclusion.

Lean's standard theorem shape introduces inputs and hypotheses before the colon and places the claim after it. 【1-bb9694】

Lesson

A proof can be correct without being easy to understand. Clarity makes the verified structure visible without changing what was proved.

5. Continuity

Question

What remains valid when the expression or system changes?

The student extends the original theorem.

For example:

  • replace double with triple;
  • move from one natural number to a list of numbers;
  • or prove that equality remains preserved through another transformation.

The learner then traces:

  • what stayed the same;
  • what definition changed;
  • which proof steps still work;
  • and where the earlier proof breaks.

Lesson

Continuity does not require the proof to remain unchanged. It asks which relationships survive the transition and which must be rebuilt.

A system can be inspected through a small set of related positions rather than treated as one undivided thing.

Where AACCC Came From

AACCC did not begin as an isolated educational checklist.

Within the TriadicFrameworks project, it emerged from a longer sequence of exploratory frameworks and formalizations:

TFT → Triads → Theorems → RTT → AACCC

This is a project-lineage interpretation. The earlier works were not originally written as five AACCC stages, and the relationships below should not be read as exact one-to-one historical derivations.

Instead, AACCC gathers recurring structural habits from that lineage into five student-facing questions:

  1. Awareness: What am I observing?
  2. Alignment: What corresponds?
  3. Coherence: Does it hold together?
  4. Clarity: Can it be understood and inspected?
  5. Continuity: What persists through change?

1. Triadic Framework for Everything — TFT

TFT introduced an early triadic way of organizing and examining systems, including complementary operators and recurring three-part structures.

For the Five Guides, its useful inheritance is:

A system can be inspected through several related positions rather than treated as one undivided thing.

Lean Interpretation

The accompanying Lean example introduces a generic three-part structure and explores how that structure behaves under mapping and composition.

This is an abstraction inspired by TFT's triadic organization.

It is not a direct formalization or verification of TFT's broader physical or cosmological proposals.

2. Nawderian Theorem

The Nawderian Theorem declares several dimension groupings centered around a shared dimension:

  • corridor dimensions: {1, 2, 6};
  • wrapping dimensions: {3, 6, 9};
  • visible dimensions: {4, 5, 6};
  • invisible dimensions: {6, 7, 8}.

A useful educational contribution is:

Declared structures should make their members, intersections, mappings, and relationships inspectable.

An Important Formalization Question

The source also describes a wrapping relationship in which the {3, 6, 9} structure contains or wraps the visible and invisible structures.

Under ordinary finite-set membership, however:

{3, 6, 9}

does not contain:

{4, 5, 6, 7, 8}.

What the declared sets demonstrably share is dimension 6.

This does not require us to discard the proposed wrapping idea.

It tells us that "wraps" needs its own defined relationship if it means something other than ordinary set containment.

That makes this an unusually useful AACCC teaching example:

Awareness: What sets were actually declared?

Alignment: Does "wraps" mean ordinary containment?

Coherence: Does the containment claim follow under that definition?

Clarity: What relationship was actually intended?

Continuity: Can the intended relationship survive a more precise formalization?

Lean Interpretation

The initial Lean model should therefore prove only the relationships supported directly by ordinary set semantics, such as membership and shared intersection.

A later exercise may define a distinct Wraps relation and investigate what properties that relation requires.

3. Nawderian SET Theorem

The SET framework organizes:

Spin → Electrolysis / field-charge behavior → Temperature

within a supplied gravitational frame.

Its useful structural contribution is:

A larger behavior can be decomposed into named contributions and recomposed into an inspectable whole.

Lean Interpretation

The Lean example models the SET triad as three named contributions and verifies their declared additive composition with a supplied gravitational contribution.

The model proves the consequences of that algebraic specification.

It does not establish that SET is a complete physical description or that its components are empirically sufficient to explain a physical system.

4. Nawderian Temperature Engine Theorem

The Temperature Engine introduces a temperature triad:

T = (T_hot, T_cold, ∇T)

and proposes a directional relationship of the general form:

F_T = -α∇T

Its useful structural contribution is:

A gradient is more than two values. It expresses a relationship and direction between them.

Lean Interpretation

The first Lean model deliberately simplifies the proposed vector relation into a one-dimensional signed algebraic example.

Within that simplified model, Lean can test consequences such as:

  • zero gradient produces zero modeled force;
  • zero coupling produces zero modeled force;
  • reversing the gradient reverses the modeled force.

Those are consequences of the supplied mathematical definition.

They are not empirical validation of the full Temperature Engine interpretation.

5. Unified Resonance Theorem

The Unified Resonance work proposes a three-factor composition:

Resonance_glyph = Q_z · F_θ · S_dim

The structural lesson useful to Five Guides is:

A result may depend on several distinguishable factors whose composition should remain visible and inspectable.

Lean Interpretation

The initial Lean example places these factors inside a common abstract multiplicative structure.

That is a modeling choice introduced by the Lean exercise.

The source does not by itself establish that the factors share a mathematical type, nor does the notation alone define all algebraic properties of their composition.

Therefore, a theorem such as associativity means:

Regrouping is valid under the algebraic assumptions supplied to the Lean model.

It does not establish associativity as an independent property of the original Unified Resonance proposal.

A more source-faithful future formalization would first define explicit types for the factors and the operation connecting them.

6. Resonance-Time Theory — RTT

RTT expands the lineage toward systems described through state, flow, resonance, operators, transitions, and behavior across updates.

For AACCC, its particularly useful contribution is:

A structure can be inspected not only as a present state, but through how its relationships persist, drift, break, or change over time.

RTT should remain identified according to its project status as a frozen historical/reference specification, rather than being presented here as independently validated theory.

Lean Interpretation

The initial Lean example creates a small transition-system model inspired by RTT's state-and-update language.

It can formalize ideas such as:

  • a declared state;
  • an update;
  • a transition;
  • preservation under a neutral update;
  • and explicitly defined continuity conditions.

Any Continuity predicate introduced in the Lean file belongs to the formal scaffold unless that exact definition can be traced directly to the frozen RTT specification.

The example therefore does not claim to formalize all of RTT.

What the Lean Examples Verify

The Lean examples formalize selected structures drawn from or inspired by the source documents.

Lean can verify that, within the supplied formal model:

  • definitions are well-formed;
  • stated conclusions follow from declared assumptions;
  • transformations preserve specified properties;
  • and proofs satisfy Lean's formal requirements.

Lean does not, merely by proving those statements, establish that:

  • the proposed model accurately describes nature;
  • its assumptions are empirically justified;
  • its variables correspond to measurable physical quantities;
  • its interpretation is unique;
  • or its broader scientific claims have been independently validated.

Formal consistency and empirical validity are different questions.

That distinction is not a limitation to hide.

It is part of the lesson.

AACCC Can Inspect Its Own Origins

These origin examples ultimately give the student an opportunity to turn the Five Guides back onto the framework itself.

Awareness

What did the original source actually claim?

Alignment

Does our formal model correspond to that claim?

Coherence

Does the conclusion follow from the definitions and assumptions?

Clarity

Have we made the model precise enough for another person to inspect?

Continuity

What survived as the idea moved from the original framework through later theorems, RTT, and finally AACCC?

This is important.

The Five Guides should not teach students merely to apply AACCC to somebody else's ideas.

AACCC should be able to inspect its own lineage too.

06_tft_origin.lean

Purpose

Introduce TFT's triadic organization through a minimal, domain-neutral three-part structure.

Proposed Structure

Triad α
├── first
├── second
└── third

Lean Elements

Define: - a generic Triad α; - a map operation applying a transformation to all three positions; - identity transformation; - composed transformations.

Proof Targets

Identity

Mapping the identity function across a triad leaves the triad unchanged.

Composition

Mapping f and then g produces the same result as mapping their composition.

Student Challenge

Given a transformation:

x ↦ x + 1

predict the result before asking Lean to evaluate it. Then compare:

map f (map g t)

with:

map (f ∘ g) t

AACCC Connection

Awareness: What are the three declared positions? Alignment: Which output corresponds to each input? Coherence: Does the mapping preserve the triadic structure? Clarity: Can the transformation be inspected component by component? Continuity: What persists after repeated transformations?

Boundary

This file formalizes a generic triadic data structure inspired by TFT. It does not formalize TFT's broader physical or cosmological claims.

07_nawderian_theorem.lean

Purpose

Formalize the explicitly declared dimension groups and expose the difference between shared membership and wrapping/containment.

Proposed Structures

Corridor  = {1, 2, 6}
Wrapping  = {3, 6, 9}
Visible   = {4, 5, 6}
Invisible = {6, 7, 8}

Lean Elements

Represent each group as a finite set. Define or prove ordinary: - membership; - intersection; - non-containment.

Proof Targets

Shared Anchor

6 belongs to each declared group.

Visible/Invisible Intersection

6 belongs to both the visible and invisible groups.

Containment Check

Test whether ordinary set semantics support:

Visible ∪ Invisible ⊆ Wrapping

They should not. The failed proposition is educationally important and should not be repaired by changing the sets.

Student Challenge

Ask:

If "wraps" does not mean ordinary set containment, what kind of relationship would need to be defined?

An optional later extension could introduce:

Wraps A B

but only after its intended semantics are explicitly stated.

AACCC Connection

Awareness: What sets were actually declared? Alignment: Which dimensions correspond or intersect? Coherence: Does the stated containment interpretation follow? Clarity: What exactly does "wraps" mean? Continuity: Can the intended relationship survive a more precise definition?

Boundary

Lean should expose the mismatch rather than silently invent semantics that make it disappear.

08_nawderian_set_theorem.lean

Purpose

Represent SET as three explicitly named contributions that participate in a larger composition.

Proposed Structure

SET
├── spin
├── electrolysis / field-charge
└── temperature

plus:

gravity

Lean Elements

Define a typed structure such as:

SETContribution

containing: - spin; - electrolysis; - temperature. Define a supplied gravitational contribution separately.

Then define:

total =
    gravity
    + spin
    + electrolysis
    + temperature

Proof Targets

Expansion

The implemented total unfolds to the declared additive specification.

Neutral Component

If one contribution is zero, the remaining contributions are preserved.

All Neutral

If every SET contribution is zero, the modeled total reduces to the supplied gravitational contribution.

Student Challenge

Remove or change one contribution and ask:

What changed in the resulting expression?

Then distinguish:

The algebra changed

from:

The physical interpretation changed.

Lean directly establishes only the first.

AACCC Connection

Awareness: What contributions have been declared? Alignment: Which term corresponds to which contribution? Coherence: Does the total contain exactly the declared terms? Clarity: Can each contribution remain visible in the expression? Continuity: What remains when one contribution changes?

Boundary

The exercise verifies properties of the supplied algebraic decomposition. It does not establish empirical sufficiency of the SET model.

09_temperature_engine_theorem.lean

Purpose

Introduce a small directional model based on temperature gradient.

Proposed Source Relationship

F_T = -α∇T

Lean Simplification

Begin with a one-dimensional signed model:

temperatureForce α gradient =
    -(α * gradient)

This deliberately replaces the fuller spatial/vector interpretation with something beginners can inspect.

Proof Targets

Zero Gradient

temperatureForce α 0 = 0

Zero Coupling

temperatureForce 0 gradient = 0

Gradient Reversal

temperatureForce α (-gradient)
    = -(temperatureForce α gradient)

under an appropriate numeric type and assumptions.

Student Challenge

Calculate the modeled result for:

gradient = 5

then:

gradient = -5

Ask:

What changed?

What remained unchanged?

AACCC Connection

Awareness: What values are actually supplied? Alignment: How does gradient direction correspond to modeled force direction? Coherence: Do the consequences follow from the definition? Clarity: Are sign and direction explicit? Continuity: What happens when the gradient changes through zero?

Boundary

The Lean model verifies consequences of the simplified one-dimensional equation. It does not establish the proposed physical interpretation of the Temperature Engine.

Proposed Source Form

    Q_z · F_θ · S_dim

11_rtt_origin.lean

Purpose

Introduce state transition and preservation using a deliberately small system inspired by RTT's state-and-update language.

Proposed Structure

Use a minimal three-part state such as:

State
├── scalar
├── flow
└── resonance

and an update:

Update
├── scalarChange
├── flowChange
└── resonanceChange

Lean Elements

Define:

applyUpdate : State → Update → State

Then introduce a small, explicitly local continuity predicate. For example:

ContinuousFrom before update after

could initially mean simply:

applyUpdate before update = after

The file must state that this predicate belongs to the Lean teaching scaffold unless an equivalent definition is traced directly to RTT.

Proof Targets

Declared Transition

Applying a known update produces the expected next state.

Neutral Update

Applying a zero update leaves the state unchanged.

Transition Witness

A recorded before/update/after sequence satisfies the local transition predicate exactly when the update produces the declared result.

Student Challenge

Change one component of the update.

What changed?

What persisted?

Does the previously proved transition still hold?

Then try a neutral update.

Awareness: What state is currently observed?
Alignment: Which update component acts on which state component?
Coherence: Does the resulting state follow from the declared update?
Clarity: Can the transition be inspected directly?
Continuity: What persists, changes, or breaks across the transition?

Boundary

This is a small transition-system scaffold inspired by RTT. It is not a formalization of the complete frozen RTT specification.

Lean proves that a formal conclusion follows from the supplied definitions and assumptions.
