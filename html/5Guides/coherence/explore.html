Coherence Explore

Does this hold together?

You've already practiced two useful questions.

Awareness asked:

What am I observing?

Alignment added:

What corresponds?

Now suppose you've carefully observed the pieces.

You've compared them.

You've found several connections.

Everything looks promising.

Then you step back and think:

Wait a minute... 🤔

Something doesn't quite fit.

Welcome to Coherence.

The "Wait a Minute" Game

Let's start with something simple.

Read these three statements:

Sam always walks to school.

Sam's bicycle has a flat tire today.

Sam cannot get to school because the bicycle has a flat tire.

Read them once more.

Anything bothering you?

The individual statements are easy to understand.

But together they create a question:

If Sam always walks to school, why does the bicycle's flat tire prevent Sam from getting there?

Maybe there is a good explanation.

Perhaps:

  • Sam planned to ride today;
  • walking is temporarily impossible;
  • "always" was an exaggeration;
  • another important detail is missing.

We don't know.

And that's the point.

You noticed that the pieces do not currently hold together.

That's Coherence beginning to work.

Don't Solve It Yet

Your brain probably wants to repair the story.

That's normal.

It might invent:

Sam injured a foot this morning.

Problem solved!

Except...

Where did that information come from?

Not the story.

You added it.

Awareness catches us again. 😉

Before repairing a Coherence problem, first locate it.

Round Two — The Helpful Shortcut

Imagine a group working on a project.

They agree:

Important changes should be reviewed by the group.

Later:

Jordan discovers an error just before the deadline.

Jordan fixes it without asking the group.

The fix improves the final project.

The group later says Jordan made the right decision.

Does this hold together?

Maybe.

This one is harder.

Look for the Tension

Compare:

Important changes should be reviewed.

with:

Jordan made an important unreviewed change
and the group considers the decision correct.

There may not be a direct contradiction.

Instead, something may be missing.

Perhaps:

Emergencies are an exception.

Or:

Correcting an error is not considered an important change.

Or:

The group values the outcome more than the process.

Or perhaps:

The stated rule and the group's actual behavior
do not completely agree.

Several explanations are possible.

We don't need to choose one yet.

First notice the tension.

Coherence Is More Than Contradiction

An obvious contradiction is easy to spot.

Coherence problems can be subtler.

They may involve:

Missing information

Hidden assumptions

Unsupported conclusions

Conflicting goals

Different meanings

Incomplete relationships

Reasonable pieces working against one another

The structure can feel wrong without one sentence being obviously false.

The Better Study Plan 📚

Imagine a student says:

I want to understand this subject,
not just memorize it.

Understanding takes time,
so I'm going to study carefully.

I won't move forward until
I completely understand each section.

If something doesn't make sense,
I go back and start the section again.

I've been working hard,
but I'm barely progressing.

Everything sounds responsible.

Study carefully.

Understand deeply.

Don't rush.

So what's the problem?

Step Back

The student's goal is:

Develop understanding

The strategy is:

Require complete understanding
before moving forward

But what if later material helps explain earlier material?

Then:

Strategy intended to support understanding

may also:

Prevent access to information
that would improve understanding

Interesting.

Neither the goal nor the strategy needed to be absurd.

The Coherence problem appeared in their relationship.

Reasonable + Reasonable Can Still Create Trouble

This is an important idea.

Suppose:

A is reasonable.

B is reasonable.

That does not automatically mean:

A + B is coherent.

The pieces interact.

Coherence asks us to inspect what happens when they do.

Quick Game — Which One Feels Off?

Read each pair.

Pair 1

I want honest feedback.

Please don't tell me anything I disagree with.

Pair 2

The software should never store personal information.

Every user must create a profile with
their name and email address.

Pair 3

The event is open to everyone.

The room can hold 20 people.

Do all three contain the same kind of problem?

Not necessarily.

Pair 1 may contain tension between two goals.

Pair 2 may contain a direct design conflict, depending on what "personal information" means.

Pair 3 may not contain a contradiction at all.

It may simply be missing information about attendance.

Coherence begins by distinguishing those situations.

The Missing Piece 🧩

Consider:

The server became slow.

A configuration file changed yesterday.

Therefore the configuration change caused the slowdown.

The conclusion is possible.

But something is missing.

We currently have:

Configuration changed
        ↓
        ?
        ↓
Server slowed

The question mark matters.

Don't Build a Bridge From Air

It is tempting to replace:

?

with:

must have caused

because the events occurred near each other.

But perhaps:

  • traffic increased;
  • storage became slow;
  • another service failed;
  • a network problem occurred;
  • the configuration change really did cause it.

We don't know yet.

A visible gap is more useful than an invented connection.

Gap or Contradiction?

Try this one:

The door remained locked for the entire hour.

The door was unlocked halfway through the hour.

That looks like contradiction.

But before deciding, ask:

Same door?

Same hour?

Same meaning of locked?

Same observer?

If yes, then the two statements cannot both hold as written.

If not, the apparent contradiction may disappear.

Coherence depends on good boundaries.

Boundary Check 🔎

Imagine someone says:

The system is offline.

Someone else says:

The system is serving requests.

Contradiction?

Maybe.

But perhaps:

Offline
=
not connected to the public Internet

while:

Serving requests
=
responding on a local network

Now both statements may be true.

The Coherence problem was actually a Clarity problem hiding inside an Alignment problem.

Welcome to structural reasoning. 😉

Same Word, Different Meaning

A shared word can create false Coherence.

For example:

Person A:
The project is finished.

Person B:
The project is not finished.

Before choosing sides, ask:

What does finished mean to each person?

Perhaps:

A:
The software works.

B:
The documentation is incomplete.

Now the disagreement looks different.

The word matched.

The meanings didn't.

Hidden Assumption Game

Read:

The password is correct.

Therefore login should succeed.

Sounds reasonable.

What might be hiding between those statements?

Perhaps:

The account exists.

The account is enabled.

The authentication service works.

No additional authentication step is required.

You don't need to list every possible condition.

The point is simpler:

The conclusion may depend on assumptions that haven't been stated.

Coherence becomes easier to inspect when those assumptions are visible.

The Assumption Ladder

Sometimes an explanation grows like this:

Claim
 ↓
Problem appears
 ↓
Add assumption
 ↓
Another problem
 ↓
Add another assumption
 ↓
Another problem
 ↓
Add another assumption

Hmm.

At some point, ask:

Are we improving the explanation?

or:

Are we protecting it from every possible challenge?

Those are not always the same thing.

Don't Fear Assumptions

Assumptions are not automatically bad.

Every model uses them.

The useful questions are:

What are they?

Are they necessary?

Are they supported?

What happens if one fails?

A visible assumption is easier to inspect than a hidden one.

The Three Boxes Game

Take any explanation and place its pieces into three boxes:

SUPPORTED

UNCERTAIN

ASSUMED

For example:

SUPPORTED
The server became slow.

SUPPORTED
The configuration changed yesterday.

UNCERTAIN
The configuration change affected performance.

ASSUMED
No other important condition changed.

Now the structure becomes easier to inspect.

Add a Fourth Box

Sometimes we need:

CONTRADICTED

Now we have:

SUPPORTED

UNCERTAIN

ASSUMED

CONTRADICTED

Try sorting the pieces of an argument into those four categories.

Don't worry if some pieces could fit more than one.

The discussion around the classification may be the useful part.

The Recipe Challenge 🍲

Imagine a recipe says:

Bake for 30 minutes.

Do not heat the oven.

Serve immediately while hot.

Something seems wrong.

Maybe the recipe assumes:

The food is already hot.

Maybe:

Bake

means something unusual.

Maybe the instructions are simply inconsistent.

Your task is not to rescue the recipe.

Your task is to notice:

These instructions need another look.

The Directions Challenge 🗺️

Someone tells you:

Drive north for two miles.

Turn left.

Continue east.

Something may be wrong.

If you're traveling north and turn left, which direction should you face?

Perhaps:

  • the original direction was wrong;
  • left was wrong;
  • east was wrong;
  • the road curves;
  • another detail is missing.

Again:

Locate before repairing.

Two-Player Coherence Game 👥

Grab a partner.

One person makes up a short explanation containing three or four statements.

Try to make every statement individually plausible while introducing one subtle Coherence problem.

Example:

The library closes at 6.

Alex arrived at 6:30.

Alex borrowed a book before leaving.

The other player asks questions.

Not:

What's the trick?

Instead:

What would need to be true for all of this to hold together?

Then switch roles.

No Partner Version

Write three statements yourself.

Hide them for a few minutes.

Then come back and inspect them as though someone else wrote them.

Ask:

Does each statement make sense?

Then:

Do they make sense together?

Those are different tests.

Build a Coherent Story

Now reverse the game.

Start with:

The library closes at 6.

Alex arrived at 6:30.

Alex borrowed a book.

Instead of silently adding an explanation, explicitly add one.

For example:

The library has a self-service locker
for reserved books after closing.

Now ask:

Did the new information repair the structure?

Good.

Then:

Was that information actually available originally?

No.

That distinction matters.

Coherence Repair Should Leave a Trace

When you repair an explanation, preserve:

Original structure

Problem found

Information added or changed

Revised structure

For example:

Original:
Alex borrowed a book after closing.

Gap:
How?

Added information:
After-hours reserved-book locker.

Revised:
Alex collected a reserved book from the
after-hours locker after the library closed.

Now the repair is inspectable.

A Coherence Repair Can Change the Story

Be careful.

Suppose the original says:

Alex borrowed a book.

You change it to:

Alex returned a book.

The timeline now works.

But you didn't repair the explanation.

You changed the event.

That may be legitimate if the original was wrong.

But record it as a correction.

Don't pretend nothing changed.

Coherence of Goals

Coherence applies to goals too.

Imagine:

Goal 1:
Finish as quickly as possible.

Goal 2:
Check every detail carefully.

Goal 3:
Use no additional time.

Each may sound desirable.

Together, they create tension.

Coherence can reveal tradeoffs hidden inside a list of individually attractive goals.

The "Everything Is Priority One" Problem

Imagine:

Security is highest priority.

Performance is highest priority.

Cost reduction is highest priority.

Ease of use is highest priority.

If everything is highest priority, what happens when the goals conflict?

The problem may not be any individual goal.

The problem may be the missing relationship among them.

Coherence often requires understanding priority and constraint.

Coherence of Rules

Rules can conflict too.

For example:

Never share account credentials.

Managers may request any employee credential
when needed.

Those rules cannot both operate without additional conditions or clarification.

A good Coherence question is:

What rule applies when these two collide?

If no answer exists, the system may contain a structural gap.

Coherence of Evidence

Suppose three sensors report:

Sensor A:
20

Sensor B:
20

Sensor C:
95

What do you do?

Do not immediately throw away Sensor C.

Ask:

  • Are the sensors measuring the same thing?
  • Do they use the same units?
  • Are they in the same location?
  • Is one malfunctioning?
  • Did the system change?

Coherence problems in evidence deserve investigation.

The Odd One Out Is Not Automatically Wrong

This is important.

If:

A agrees with B

and:

C disagrees

it is tempting to conclude:

C is wrong.

Maybe.

But perhaps A and B share the same fault.

Or C observes another boundary.

Or the system changed between readings.

Preserve the mismatch until you understand it.

Alignment taught us this.

Coherence needs it too.

Coherence and Models

A model may explain:

Observation 1

Observation 2

Observation 3

Then a new observation arrives:

Observation 4

and doesn't fit.

Possible responses include:

Observation 4 is wrong.

Model needs revision.

Boundary changed.

New regime appeared.

Information is missing.

Do not choose automatically.

The mismatch is the starting point.

A Useful Phrase

When something breaks the current explanation, try saying:

This observation is not currently explained by the model.

That is much more precise than:

This observation is impossible.

The first leaves room for inspection.

Coherence and Formal Proof

Formal systems make this especially clear.

Suppose you declare:

A
B

and want:

C

If C does not follow, a proof checker will not close the goal merely because C seems plausible.

Something is missing.

Maybe:

  • another assumption;
  • another relationship;
  • another proof step;
  • a different theorem.

The formal environment makes the gap visible.

Formal Isn't Magic

A theorem can be perfectly coherent under assumptions that are not empirically justified.

For example:

Assume all zorps are blue.

Assume Mira is a zorp.

Therefore Mira is blue.

The reasoning can be valid.

The existence and properties of zorps remain another question.

Formal Coherence and empirical support are different layers.

Coherence and AI 🤖

AI-generated explanations can make wonderful Coherence exercises.

Give AI a problem.

Then ignore whether the answer sounds confident.

Instead ask:

What are the claims?

What supports each claim?

Which connections are explicit?

Which are assumed?

Where does uncertainty disappear?

Does the conclusion go beyond the evidence?

Now you're examining the structure of the response.

Ask AI Not to Fix It

One especially useful prompt is:

Find where this explanation stops holding together, but do not repair it yet.

That encourages diagnosis before solution.

Then ask:

What information would you need before trying to repair it?

That makes AI a Coherence partner rather than a conclusion generator.

Coherence and Your Own Thinking

The hardest Coherence problems may be your own.

Once we like an explanation, we naturally want to preserve it.

That can lead to:

Evidence
   ↓
Explanation

New evidence
   ↓
Extra assumption

More evidence
   ↓
Another exception

At some point, pause.

Ask:

Am I improving the explanation?

or:

Am I making it impossible to challenge?

That's a powerful Coherence check.

Make Your Explanation Vulnerable

A useful exercise is:

What observation would show that my explanation is wrong or incomplete?

If the answer is:

Nothing could.

then the explanation may be difficult to test meaningfully.

Coherence benefits from knowing where a model could fail.

"I Don't Know Yet" Fits Here Too

Suppose:

Observation A
Observation B

both appear reliable.

But you cannot yet make them fit.

You can say:

I don't know yet how these observations relate.

That is a coherent statement about your current knowledge.

You do not need to invent a bridge.

Build the Habit

When something feels wrong, pause.

Try:

What are the pieces?

What are the relationships?

What fits?

What doesn't?

What is missing?

What am I assuming?

What remains uncertain?

Then decide whether repair is even appropriate.
