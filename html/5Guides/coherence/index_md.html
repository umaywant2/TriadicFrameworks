Coherence

Does this hold together?

You have reached the third Guide.

So far, you have practiced two questions:

Awareness
What am I observing?

Alignment
What corresponds?

Now we add:

Does this hold together?

Welcome to Coherence.

The Pieces Can Be Right and the Whole Can Still Be Wrong

This is the tricky part.

Imagine assembling something.

Every individual piece looks correct.

Every connection seems reasonable.

Then you step back and discover:

Something doesn't work.

That moment is where Coherence begins.

Coherence asks us to inspect not only the pieces, but the relationships they create together.

The Basic Shape

A simple structure may look like:

A
 ↓
B
 ↓
C

Awareness asks:

What are A, B, and C?

Alignment asks:

How do they correspond?

Coherence asks:

Does the complete relationship make sense?

That may sound simple.

It can become surprisingly interesting.

Start With a Small Example

Consider:

Sam always walks to school.

Sam's bicycle has a flat tire today.

Sam cannot get to school because the bicycle has a flat tire.

Each sentence is understandable.

But together?

Something feels off.

If Sam always walks, why would the bicycle prevent the trip?

Maybe:

  • the first statement is incomplete;
  • Sam planned to ride today;
  • walking is temporarily impossible;
  • an important condition is missing;
  • or the final conclusion does not follow.

We do not know yet.

The first Coherence skill is simply noticing:

These pieces do not currently hold together.

That "Wait a Minute" Feeling

You already use Coherence.

It is the little mental pause when something makes you think:

Hang on...

Or:

Something doesn't add up.

Or:

How did we get from there to here?

That reaction is useful.

Do not rush past it.

Investigate it.

Coherence Is Not Agreement

Coherence does not mean:

Everyone agrees.

Two people can disagree while each person's reasoning remains internally coherent.

Likewise, several people can agree on something incoherent.

Agreement is social.

Coherence is structural.

Coherence Is Not Truth

This distinction matters too.

A story can be internally coherent and fictional.

A mathematical model can be coherent under assumptions that do not describe reality.

An explanation can hold together logically while beginning from a false premise.

So Coherence asks:

Do these pieces and relationships hold together?

Evidence asks an additional question:

Are the pieces themselves well supported?

We need both.

A Trickier Example

Imagine a group project.

The group agrees:

Important changes should be reviewed by the group.

Later:

Jordan discovers an error shortly before the deadline.

Jordan fixes the error without asking.

The change improves the final project.

The group says Jordan made the right decision.

Nothing here is obviously absurd.

The change worked.

The result improved.

The group approved afterward.

But now compare:

Important changes require review.

with:

This important unreviewed change was the right decision.

Do those hold together?

Maybe.

Perhaps:

  • emergencies are an exception;
  • error correction is not considered an important change;
  • the rule was never absolute;
  • the group's values changed;
  • the result matters more than the process;
  • or the stated rule and actual behavior are in tension.

The interesting part is finding what must be true for the whole structure to work.

Don't Repair It Yet

When you find a Coherence problem, the instinct may be:

Fix it.

Pause first.

Ask:

Where does the structure stop holding together?

Then:

Why?

A problem can contain useful information.

If you repair too quickly, you may erase the clue.

Missing Information

Sometimes a structure looks incoherent simply because something is missing.

For example:

A
 ↓
?
 ↓
C

You might be tempted to invent B.

Don't.

Record:

A:
known

C:
known

Relationship:
missing or uncertain

Then ask:

What observation would help fill the gap?

Missing information is not the same as contradiction.

Contradiction

A contradiction is stronger.

Consider:

The door remained locked for the entire hour.

The same door was unlocked halfway through that hour.

If:

  • the same door is meant;
  • the same hour is meant;
  • the meaning of locked is the same;

then the statements do not both hold as written.

But notice what we had to check first.

Boundary.

Time.

Meaning.

Identity.

Coherence depends on the earlier Guides.

Unsupported Conclusion

Another problem looks like:

Observation A
Observation B
      ↓
Conclusion C

But nothing actually establishes the arrow.

For example:

The website became slow.

A configuration file changed yesterday.

Therefore the configuration change caused the slowdown.

The configuration change may be responsible.

But the relationship has not yet been established.

A more coherent current statement may be:

The configuration change is one possible cause worth investigating.

That preserves the observation without overstating the evidence.

Hidden Assumptions

Sometimes the missing relationship is supplied by an assumption.

For example:

The battery is charged.

The device is connected.

Therefore the device should start.

Perhaps.

But the reasoning may also require:

The device itself is functional.

Now the structure becomes easier to inspect:

Battery charged
+
Connected correctly
+
Device functional
      ↓
Expected to start

The assumption was already doing work.

Clarity simply made it visible.

Coherence Detective 🕵️

When something does not quite fit, ask:

What are the important pieces?

How are they connected?

Which relationships are supported?

Where is the tension?

Is something missing?

Is there a contradiction?

Is a conclusion unsupported?

Is an assumption hidden?

What remains uncertain?

You do not need every question every time.

Use the ones that help locate the structural problem.

Local vs. Global Coherence

A piece can work perfectly by itself while causing trouble inside a larger system.

Imagine three software components.

Sensor
works correctly.

Processor
works correctly.

Display
works correctly.

Great.

Now discover:

Sensor:
reports Celsius

Processor:
interprets Fahrenheit

Display:
labels result as Celsius

Each component may satisfy its own local rules.

The system as a whole does not.

That is a global Coherence problem.

A System Can Pass Locally and Fail Globally

This happens outside software too.

For example:

Department A
optimizes for speed.

Department B
optimizes for precision.

Department C
optimizes for minimum cost.

Each goal may be reasonable.

Together, they may require tradeoffs.

Coherence asks:

Can these local goals operate together inside the larger system?

That question is different from asking whether each goal makes sense individually.

Coherence and Constraints

Sometimes a structure becomes difficult because several constraints compete.

Imagine:

Keep the answer under 100 words.

Include every technical detail.

Explain every assumption.

Provide three examples.

Include full historical context.

Each request makes sense.

Together, they may not.

That is a Coherence problem involving constraints.

The system may need:

  • prioritization;
  • another boundary;
  • a changed requirement;
  • or acceptance that all goals cannot be maximized simultaneously.

Coherence Can Depend on Conditions

A structure may be coherent under one condition and not another.

Consider:

Goal:
Everyone gets individual instruction.

Class size:
5 learners.

Reasonable.

Now:

Goal:
Everyone gets individual instruction.

Class size:
500 learners.

Instructor count:
1.

The goal did not change.

The conditions did.

Coherence can therefore depend on context.

The Study Plan Example

Consider:

I want to understand the subject rather than memorize it.

Understanding takes time.

I will not move forward until I completely understand each section.

When something is unclear, I restart the section.

I am working hard but barely progressing.

Every statement seems reasonable.

But perhaps the strategy is working against the goal.

What if later material helps explain earlier material?

What if temporary uncertainty is normal?

What if complete understanding is not required before progress?

Nothing had to be an obvious contradiction.

The Coherence problem appeared in the relationships among the pieces.

A Coherence Habit

When several reasonable ideas appear together, ask:

What do these ideas do when they interact?

That is often more useful than evaluating each one separately.

Don't Force a Story

Humans are good at making stories.

Sometimes too good.

We see:

A happened.

Then B happened.

Then C happened.

and quickly produce:

A caused B,
which caused C.

Maybe.

But sequence is not automatically explanation.

Coherence should help us inspect the relationship, not manufacture one.

Preserve the Gap

Sometimes the strongest current model is:

A
 ↓
?

B

That question mark can be honest.

It tells us:

A relationship may exist, but we have not established it yet.

Do not underestimate the value of a visible gap.

Coherence and Alignment

Alignment asks:

What corresponds?

Coherence asks:

Do those correspondences work together?

For example:

A ↔ B
B ↔ C
A ↔ C

Each mapping may appear reasonable independently.

But together, they may produce:

  • contradiction;
  • redundancy;
  • incompatible meaning;
  • or an unstated assumption.

So Coherence operates on the structure created by Alignment.

Coherence and Awareness

Before asking whether a structure holds together:

What did we actually observe?

An incoherent explanation may be caused by an earlier observation error.

Or the evidence may be correct while the interpretation is not.

Sometimes Coherence sends you backward:

Coherence
    ↓
Alignment
    ↓
Awareness

That's not failure.

That's inspection.

Coherence and Uncertainty

A coherent explanation does not need to answer every question.

It can include:

Known:
...

Supported:
...

Unknown:
...

Uncertain:
...

In fact, a model that honestly preserves uncertainty may be more coherent than one that pretends every gap has already been resolved.

Coherence and Repair

Once you locate a structural problem, repair may involve:

  • changing a mapping;
  • adding missing information;
  • removing an unsupported conclusion;
  • exposing an assumption;
  • clarifying a definition;
  • changing a boundary;
  • revising the model;
  • leaving a relationship unresolved.

Repair is not always:

Make the original conclusion survive.

Sometimes the conclusion should change.

When Not to Repair

Suppose two observations do not yet fit.

You do not know whether:

  • one is wrong;
  • both are incomplete;
  • a mapping is wrong;
  • a condition changed;
  • a boundary differs;
  • information is missing.

Do not invent an explanation just to restore Coherence.

Record:

These observations do not currently fit under the available explanation.

That may be exactly the correct result.

Coherence and Evidence

Consider:

Assumption:
All red objects are magnetic.

Observation:
This object is red.

Conclusion:
This object is magnetic.

The reasoning can be coherent under the supplied assumption.

But is the assumption supported?

That is a separate question.

This difference matters greatly in formal reasoning.

A proof may be valid inside its assumptions while the assumptions remain empirically unsupported.

Coherence in Formal Systems

The optional Lean path makes Coherence especially visible.

A formal theorem has something like:

Definitions
     +
Assumptions
     ↓
Claim

Lean asks whether the Claim follows.

If it does not, the proof obligation remains.

That can expose:

  • a missing assumption;
  • an unsupported target;
  • a bad definition;
  • or an incomplete proof.

But Lean still checks the formal model you gave it.

It does not independently validate the real-world truth of the premises.

A Formal Coherence Question

For any formal theorem, ask:

What was declared?

What was assumed?

What conclusion follows?

What conclusion does not follow?

Those questions are useful even if you never write a line of Lean.

Coherence in AI Answers 🤖

AI can produce explanations that sound beautifully organized.

That doesn't guarantee they hold together.

When reading an AI answer, ask:

What are its main claims?

What evidence supports them?

Which relationships connect them?

What assumptions are required?

Does any conclusion go beyond the evidence?

Did the meaning change during the explanation?

The AI response itself becomes a Coherence exercise.

Confidence Does Not Create Coherence

An explanation does not become stronger because it is written confidently.

Likewise:

Long answer

does not necessarily mean:

Coherent answer

And:

Technical language

does not automatically mean:

Supported reasoning

Inspect the relationships.

Coherence Can Be Partial

Not every structure must receive a binary judgment:

Coherent

or:

Incoherent

You may find:

Partly coherent

Coherent under condition X

Coherent within boundary Y

Uncertain because relationship Z is missing

Internally coherent but empirically unsupported

Those can be stronger descriptions because they preserve what you actually know.

A Useful Coherence Scale

For practice, you can use:

Coherent

Partly Coherent

Conditional

Uncertain

Incoherent

Do not treat this as a numerical scoring system.

It is simply a vocabulary for describing the current structural judgment.

Coherence Can Change

A structure that appears coherent today may become less coherent when new information arrives.

For example:

Current evidence
      ↓
Explanation fits

Then:

New observation
      ↓
Explanation no longer fits

That does not mean the earlier reasoning was dishonest.

It means the available structure changed.

This begins our bridge toward Continuity.

Mastery Goal

By the end of Coherence, you should substantially be able to:

  • identify the important pieces of a structure;
  • inspect relationships among them;
  • recognize contradiction;
  • recognize missing relationships;
  • identify unsupported conclusions;
  • expose important assumptions;
  • distinguish local from global Coherence;
  • preserve uncertainty;
  • locate where the structure stops holding together;
  • reconsider the structure after revision.

The goal is practical reasoning.

Not vocabulary recall.

They stare at you.

The structure might be coherent.

But can another person see it?

That's a new problem.

The next question is:

Can this be understood well enough to inspect?
