Clarity Explore

Can someone else see what you mean?

You have already practiced three questions:

Awareness
What am I observing?

Alignment
What corresponds?

Coherence
Does this hold together?

Now imagine you've done all three.

You observed carefully.

You checked the relationships.

The pieces hold together.

You understand the idea perfectly.

Then you explain it to someone else...

and they stare at you.

😐

Welcome to Clarity.

The Curse of Knowing What You Mean

Here's the problem.

When you already understand something, your mind quietly fills in missing pieces.

You know:

  • what "it" refers to;
  • which direction "over there" means;
  • what happened between step 2 and step 3;
  • which definition you are using;
  • why the conclusion makes sense.

Someone else may know none of that.

So an explanation can feel perfectly clear from the inside...

while looking like a puzzle from the outside.

Quick Game — Terrible Directions 🗺️

Imagine someone tells you:

Go down there, turn where that place used to be, and it's just past the thing on the left.

Ready?

Go! 😄

Probably not.

Let's inspect the directions.

What does:

there

mean?

Which:

place

used to be there?

What:

thing

is on the left?

And left from which direction?

The person giving the directions may know exactly what they mean.

The information simply did not make it into the explanation.

Clarity Is Shared Visibility

A useful way to think about Clarity is:

What I Mean
     ↓
What I Express
     ↓
What You Can Inspect

The closer those relationships become, the easier the idea is to work with together.

Clarity does not require two people to agree.

It gives them enough shared structure to understand what they are agreeing or disagreeing about.

The Drawing Game ✏️

Choose a simple object.

Do not name it.

Describe how to draw it.

For example, instead of:

Draw a house.

try:

Draw a square.

Place a triangle directly above the square.

Draw a small rectangle near the bottom center
of the square.

If you have a partner, let them follow the instructions.

No hints once they begin.

Then compare the drawing with what you imagined.

No Partner? No Problem

Write your drawing instructions.

Put them aside for a little while.

Then return and follow them as literally as possible.

Pretend you no longer know what you intended.

Ask:

Which steps depended on information that never appeared in the instructions?

That's the Clarity gap.

The "You Know What I Mean" Challenge

Read:

Put it where the other one was,
after you finish that part.

You might know exactly what this means in context.

Another person may need:

What is "it"?

What is "the other one"?

Where was it?

What is "that part"?

What counts as finished?

Sometimes Clarity is simply replacing invisible context with visible context.

More Words Are Not Automatically Clearer

Let's fix the vague instruction by adding detail:

Take the object that you were using earlier,
which is probably still near the other objects,
and put it in the place where the previous object
had been before you moved that one during the earlier step.

Well...

We added words. 😄

Did we add Clarity?

Not much.

Fewer Words Are Not Automatically Clearer Either

Try:

Move it.

Beautifully concise.

Completely mysterious.

Clarity is not:

Longer

or:

Shorter

It is:

Enough visible structure
for the reader to inspect what matters

Before and After 🔎

Consider:

The project didn't work because communication
wasn't good and some people were doing different
things because they understood the plan differently,
although everyone thought they understood what
the group was trying to do.

There is probably a useful idea inside that sentence.

Let's make the structure visible.

A Clearer Version

The group agreed on the goal.

Members interpreted the plan differently.

Because those differences were not noticed,
people began working in different directions.

Now we can see:

Shared Goal
     ↓
Different Interpretations
     ↓
Different Actions

Same basic idea.

Much easier to inspect.

But Wait...

Did we preserve everything?

The original also said:

everyone thought they understood

That may matter.

Perhaps a better revision is:

The group believed they shared an understanding
of the goal and plan.

In practice, members interpreted the plan differently.

Because those differences were not noticed,
people began working in different directions.

Now another piece of the original meaning has survived.

This is the important Clarity habit:

Don't stop when the rewrite sounds better.

Ask:

Did the meaning survive?

Meaning Drift

Watch what happens here.

Version 1

The evidence may support the explanation.

Version 2

The evidence supports the explanation.

Version 3

The evidence confirms the explanation.

The sentences became more confident.

Did they become clearer?

Perhaps.

Did they retain the same meaning?

No.

Something drifted.

Clarity Should Preserve Uncertainty

Words such as:

may

possibly

suggests

under these conditions

not yet established

insufficient information

may look like clutter.

Sometimes they are essential.

If uncertainty belongs to the claim, removing it does not improve Clarity.

It changes the claim.

Clear Uncertainty

Compare:

We're not sure.

with:

We observed A and B.

We do not yet know whether A caused B.

Observation C would help test that relationship.

Both express uncertainty.

The second tells us:

what is known

what is unknown

what relationship is uncertain

what could help

Uncertainty can be clear.

Ambiguity Game

Read:

Alex told Jordan that their result was wrong.

Whose result?

  • Alex's?
  • Jordan's?
  • a shared result?

The sentence may support several interpretations.

That's ambiguity.

Fix It Two Ways

Try writing two clear versions.

Meaning 1

Alex told Jordan that Jordan's result was wrong.

Meaning 2

Alex told Jordan that Alex's result was wrong.

Both could be valid interpretations of the original.

Clarity required deciding which one was intended.

Same Word, Different Meaning

Consider:

The project is finished.

Someone else says:

The project is not finished.

Contradiction?

Maybe.

Ask what:

finished

means.

Perhaps:

Person A:
The code works.

Person B:
The tests and documentation are incomplete.

The shared word was hiding different meanings.

Clarity makes the difference visible.

Define the Important Word

Try:

Stable

Pick one.

Complete:

In this problem, I mean:

Now use the word again.

Did the definition change how inspectable the statement became?

The Hidden Step Game

Read:

The service was slow.

Therefore we increased database capacity.

Maybe that decision was sensible.

But something is missing.

Perhaps the actual reasoning was:

Service became slow
      ↓
Database latency increased
      ↓
Database resource saturation was observed
      ↓
Capacity increase was selected

Now the decision is easier to inspect.

Don't Invent the Hidden Step

Careful.

If all you actually know is:

Service was slow.

Database capacity was increased.

you cannot quietly add:

Database saturation was measured.

just because it makes the story coherent.

Instead say:

Missing:
The evidence connecting the slowdown
to the database-capacity decision
has not been provided.

That's Clarity too.

The Missing Step Can Be the Result

Sometimes the clearest conclusion is:

We cannot yet see how the conclusion follows.

That is better than inventing a bridge.

Technical Before and After 💻

Consider:

The script processes files from the input directory
and if there's a problem it doesn't process them
and writes the problem to the log unless logging
isn't enabled.

What exactly happens?

You may immediately wonder:

  • Does one failed file stop everything?
  • Is only that file skipped?
  • Does processing continue?
  • What happens when logging is disabled?
  • What does "them" refer to?

The behavior is buried.

Make the Structure Visible

We could begin with:

For each input file:

1. Attempt to process the file.

2. If processing succeeds:
   continue to the next step.

3. If processing fails:
   behavior after failure is not specified.

4. If logging is enabled:
   record the failure.

Notice what we did not do.

We did not invent:

skip the file and continue

because the original never clearly said that.

We exposed the missing behavior.

Clarity Can Reveal Missing Design

This is an important technical lesson.

Sometimes unclear documentation is not merely unclear writing.

The underlying behavior may never have been decided.

When you try to document:

What happens after failure?

you may discover:

Nobody defined it.

That's not just a writing problem.

Clarity exposed a design problem.

Quick Technical Game — What's Missing?

Read:

If validation fails, handle the error appropriately.

😄

Ask:

What counts as failure?

What does "handle" mean?

Who handles it?

What is "appropriately"?

Does execution continue?

Is the user notified?

Is the event logged?

The sentence may be fine as a high-level placeholder.

It is not a complete operational specification.

Clarity depends on purpose.

Clear to Whom?

Imagine that sentence appears in:

Executive overview

Maybe it's sufficient.

Now imagine it appears in:

Implementation specification

Probably not.

Clarity depends on audience and task.

Ask:

Who needs to use this?

Then:

What do they need to be able to do with it?

Audience Challenge

Explain:

A software state transition

to:

A first-time student

Now explain it to:

A software developer

The wording will probably change.

The essential meaning should not.

Clarity adapts presentation while preserving structure.

Representation Matters

Some ideas become clearer when they change form.

For example:

The request enters the system, validation occurs,
and if validation succeeds processing continues;
otherwise an error is returned.

Could become:

Request
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Valid?
 ┌─┴─┐
Yes  No
 ↓    ↓
Process Error

Same basic structure.

Different representation.

A Diagram Is Not Automatically Clearer

A diagram can also hide information.

Perhaps:

Error

actually contains several possible outcomes.

The picture may look neat because details disappeared.

So after changing representation, ask:

What became easier to see?

Then:

What became harder to see?

Then:

What disappeared?

The Table Challenge

Suppose three sensors report:

Sensor A reports 20.

Sensor B reports 20.

Sensor C reports 95.

A table may help:

Sensor Reading
A 20
B 20
C 95

Better.

But still incomplete.

What units?

Where are the sensors?

When were they measured?

Add the Missing Context

Now:

Sensor Reading Unit Location
A 20 °C Room
B 20 °C Room
C 95 °C Exhaust vent

Whoa.

The apparent mismatch changed meaning.

Sensor C may not be the odd one out anymore.

Clarity changed what we could inspect.

Labels Matter

Compare:

20
20
95

with:

Room Sensor A: 20 °C

Room Sensor B: 20 °C

Exhaust Sensor C: 95 °C

The values did not change.

The labels changed our understanding.

Clarity often comes from relationships around the data, not changes to the data itself.

Units Matter Too

Imagine:

Distance = 5

Five what?

Now:

Distance = 5 km

Much better.

Units are part of meaning.

Leaving them out can create false Alignment and poor Coherence.

Clarity and Sequence

Read:

Turn off the computer.

Save your work.

Hmm.

Each instruction is understandable.

The sequence may be a problem.

Clarity includes order when order matters.

Clarity and Conditions

Compare:

Restart the service.

with:

If configuration validation succeeds,
restart the service.

The condition matters.

Removing it changes the instruction.

Clarity and Exceptions

Compare:

Never delete archived files.

with:

Never delete archived files,
except expired test archives after review.

The exception is part of the rule.

If it remains hidden, different people may follow different versions of the policy.

Clarity Is Not Decoration

A page can have:

  • beautiful colors;
  • perfect spacing;
  • lovely typography;
  • icons;
  • polished headings.

And still say something unclear.

Formatting can support Clarity.

It cannot substitute for it.

Clarity Is Not Fancy Vocabulary

Compare:

We need to check whether these values match.

with:

A multidimensional reconciliation of the
relevant quantitative modalities must be
operationalized.

Well then. 😄

Technical language has its place.

Use it when the concept needs it.

Not when the sentence merely wants to look important.

Clarity Is Not Dumbing Down

Some ideas really are difficult.

Clarity does not mean pretending they are simple.

Instead:

Complicated idea
      ↓
Visible pieces
      ↓
Visible relationships
      ↓
Inspectable complexity

The complexity can remain.

We are untangling it, not erasing it.

The Headphone Knot 🎧

Think of tangled headphones.

Clarity does not mean:

Remove half the wire.

It means:

This loop goes here.

This wire crosses there.

This is the knot.

This end connects to that side.

The headphones remain complicated.

The structure becomes easier to see.

Clarity Detective 🔎

When something is difficult to follow, ask:

What is being claimed?

What does this term mean here?

What information is missing?

What step is unstated?

What assumption is hidden?

What remains uncertain?

Would another representation help?

What must not change?

That last question protects meaning during revision.

Clarity and Provenance

Knowing where a claim came from can improve Clarity.

For example:

Source says:
...

Our interpretation:
...

Our revision:
...

Now a reader can distinguish the source from what we added.

This is especially important when summarizing someone else's work.

Clarity and AI 🤖

AI can be an excellent rewriting partner.

It can also make an uncertain statement sound beautifully certain.

Try this prompt:

Rewrite this more clearly without adding new claims. Then list what you changed.

Then inspect the result.

Ask:

Did any qualification disappear?

Did any claim become stronger?

Did any important uncertainty vanish?

Did the AI insert an explanation the source never gave?

The rewrite itself becomes a Clarity exercise.

Watch the Confidence Ladder

An AI may turn:

may support

into:

supports

then into:

demonstrates

then into:

proves

Those are not stylistic synonyms.

They express different strengths of claim.

Clarity requires preserving that distinction.

Ask AI to Show Its Changes

A useful follow-up is:

Compare your revision with my original and list every meaningful change.

Then inspect that list.

The AI may catch its own drift.

It may not.

You still make the final comparison.

Clarity and Formal Proof

Formal systems take Clarity in another direction.

A proof assistant may force us to specify:

Objects

Types

Definitions

Assumptions

Goal

Proof

That can expose relationships that ordinary prose allows us to leave vague.

But formal notation creates another audience problem.

A proof can be precise and still difficult for a learner to understand.

Two Kinds of Clarity

We may need both:

Formal Clarity

and:

Explanatory Clarity

For example:

Lean theorem
      ↕
Plain-language explanation

The formal theorem gives precision.

The explanation provides accessibility.

The mapping between them needs inspection too.

A Formal Example

Suppose Lean expresses:

a = b

and then proves that a transformation preserves the relationship.

A student-friendly explanation might say:

If the two starting values are equal, applying the same transformation to each preserves their equality.

Now ask:

Did the explanation preserve the formal claim?

That's Clarity across representations.
