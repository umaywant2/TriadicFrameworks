Student Footprint

Leave something behind only if you want to.

You do not need to identify yourself to learn through 5Guides.

You do not need to create a public profile simply because you completed a Guide.

And choosing not to leave feedback does not change what you accomplished.

The Student Footprint exists for one simple reason:

Some learners may want to quietlyay, "I was here, and this was my experience."**

A Footprint, Not a Profile

This is not intended to become a social network.

There are no:

  • follower counts;
  • popularity rankings;
  • public student profiles;
  • required biographies;
  • engagement scores;
  • competitive leaderboards;
  • or advertising profiles.

A Student Footprint should remain deliberately small.

The basic footprint contains only:

Where are you learning from?

[ Optional country ]

One thought about your experience

[ Optional short reflection ]

Both are optional.

Why Country?

The purpose isn't to determine who you are.

It is simply to allow learners, if they choose, to leave a broad geographical signal showing that the Guides are being encountered in different places.

The country should be selected by the learner.

It should not need to be inferred from:

  • an IP address;
  • browser information;
  • device information;
  • location services;
  • or another hidden signal.

If you would rather not provide a country, leave it blank.

Why a Short Reflection?

Sometimes the most useful feedback is small.

For example:

"I noticed how quickly I turned an assumption into a fact."

Or:

"Changing viewpoints helped me understand the blind-spot exercise."

Or:

"I didn't understand Alignment until I stopped trying to make everything match."

The goal isn't to collect testimonials.

The goal is simply to leave a small voluntary trace of the learning experience.

What We Don't Need

A Student Footprint should not require:

  • your name;
  • your email address;
  • your phone number;
  • your school;
  • your employer;
  • your age;
  • your gender;
  • your social-media account;
  • or another unnecessary personal identifier.

If the Footprint can work without knowing something about you, we should prefer not knowing it.

Learning Comes First

The order matters.

Learn
  ↓
Practice
  ↓
Demonstrate
  ↓
Badge
  ↓
Reflect
  ↓
Optional Footprint

The Footprint comes after the learning milestone.

That separation is intentional.

Your decision to leave feedback should not affect:

  • whether you can complete the Guide;
  • whether you can attempt mastery;
  • whether you receive the badge;
  • whether you can continue;
  • or whether your learning "counts."

Feedback should never become the price of completion.

If You Leave Nothing

That's completely fine.

Your path may simply be:

Learn
  ↓
Practice
  ↓
Demonstrate
  ↓
Badge
  ↓
Continue

No Footprint required.

No explanation required.

No "Are you sure?" screen required.

You came to learn.

You learned.

You continued.

That's enough.

If You Do Leave Something

A minimal submission might look like:

Country:
Canada

Reflection:
"The second observation exercise made me notice
how much I was filling in myself."

Or:

Country:
[not provided]

Reflection:
"I finally understood why uncertainty can be useful."

Or simply:

Country:
Japan

Reflection:
[not provided]

A learner should be able to provide either field without being required to provide the other.

Public Footprints

If Student Footprints are eventually displayed publicly, they should remain quiet and simple.

For example:

🇨🇦
"I started noticing where my assumptions entered the problem."

🇯🇵
"Going back to my original observation was the useful part."

🇺🇸
"I learned that a mismatch doesn't always need repairing."

The purpose is not:

Look how many learners we have!

The purpose is closer to:

Someone else passed through here too.

Don't Turn the Board Into a Scoreboard

The Student Footprint began from the idea of a community corkboard.

That's worth preserving.

Imagine a board near the door where someone can pin a small note.

You may read it.

You may leave one.

You may walk past it.

Nobody follows you around the building because you looked at the board.

That's the spirit of the Student Footprint.

So avoid turning it into:

  • a popularity contest;
  • a ranking system;
  • a competition between countries;
  • a measure of student worth;
  • or a growth metric presented back to the learner.

A collection of Footprints should remain a collection of voluntary traces, not a scoreboard.

Small Counts Can Reveal People

Privacy becomes especially important when only a few learners have contributed from a particular place.

For example, displaying:

"One learner from a particular location completed Continuity today."

could reveal more than we intended when combined with other information.

So public presentation should favor broad, minimal information over detailed slices.

The design principle is:

If a statistic makes a person easier to identify, we probably don't need that statistic.

Reflection Moderation

A free-text reflection creates responsibilities that a country selector does not.

Students might accidentally submit:

  • their full name;
  • an email address;
  • a phone number;
  • a school;
  • a workplace;
  • another person's information;
  • or something they later regret sharing publicly.

So the interface should remind learners:

Please don't include personal or identifying information in your reflection.

If reflections are displayed publicly, they should be treated as submissions requiring appropriate review and safety controls before publication.

A learner choosing to submit text does not mean every submitted detail must automatically become public.

Keep the Reflection Small

The Footprint should not slowly become a comment system.

A short reflection encourages:

What is one thing you noticed?

rather than:

Tell us everything about yourself and your experience.

That keeps the feature closer to its original purpose:

a Post-it note, not an account.

Country Wording

Prefer:

Where are you learning from?

rather than:

Country of origin

"Country of origin" can mean:

  • birthplace;
  • nationality;
  • ancestry;
  • citizenship;
  • or family background.

None of those are necessary here.

We only need the broad location the learner voluntarily chooses to associate with the learning experience.

The selector should also support:

Prefer not to say

or simply allow the field to remain empty.

Footprint Design

A simple interface could be:

Leave a Footprint

Where are you learning from?
[ Optional country selector ]

One thought about your experience:
[ Optional short reflection ]

Please don't include personal or identifying
information in your reflection.

[ Leave Footprint ]

or

[ Continue without leaving one ]

The second choice should be just as easy as the first.

No Dark Patterns

The interface should not pressure the learner into participating.

Avoid:

"No thanks, I don't want to help."

Prefer:

Continue without leaving a Footprint

Avoid repeatedly asking after the learner declines.

Avoid hiding the skip option.

Avoid suggesting that leaving feedback is necessary to support the project.

Consent works best when no is easy.

Badge and Footprint Must Remain Separate

The badge represents:

The learner completed the Guide and demonstrated the defined skill.

The Footprint represents:

The learner voluntarily chose to leave a small piece of feedback.

Those should never become confused.

A learner with:

Badge = yes
Footprint = no

has completed exactly the same learning milestone as someone with:

Badge = yes
Footprint = yes

The Footprint adds no academic status.

No Individual Footprint Counter

Avoid attaching statistics to individual reflections such as:

  • likes;
  • views;
  • reactions;
  • helpful votes;
  • popularity;
  • shares.

The moment an individual Footprint starts accumulating engagement data, the quiet corkboard begins turning into a social platform.

That's not what we're building.

Aggregate Learning Signals

If aggregate information is eventually useful, keep it broad.

For example:

Learners have voluntarily left Footprints from multiple countries.

Or display the participating countries without ranking them.

If counts are ever shown, they should be justified by a clear student-facing purpose rather than collected simply because they are available.

The default should remain:

Collect less.

Deletion and Correction

A privacy-respecting Footprint system should anticipate a simple human reality:

Someone may change their mind.

Implementation planning should therefore include a way to handle:

  • accidental submissions;
  • inappropriate personal information;
  • correction requests;
  • removal requests;
  • and moderation decisions.

The exact mechanism belongs to the later implementation plan.

The principle belongs here:

Voluntary participation should not become permanent participation simply because someone clicked Submit once.

Data Minimization

Before adding any field, ask:

Do we actually need this?

If the answer is merely:

"It might be interesting someday."

don't collect it.

For the initial Student Footprint, the target remains:

Optional country
+
Optional short reflection

Nothing else should be added without a clear reason.

What About Technical Server Data?

A web service may require some technical information simply to receive and protect a request.

That implementation question should be handled separately and documented honestly when the Footprint service is built.

The Student Footprint promise should therefore avoid claiming:

No information ever reaches a server.

unless the eventual implementation can actually guarantee that.

The stronger and more honest principle is:

Do not intentionally build unnecessary identity, behavioral, or tracking data into the Footprint system.

Implementation details should be disclosed once they exist.

What the Footprint Is Not

The Student Footprint is not intended to prove:

  • how many people visited 5Guides;
  • how many people completed a Guide;
  • what percentage of visitors became learners;
  • where every visitor came from;
  • how long people remained on a page;
  • what route they took through the material;
  • or who returned later.

Those are analytics questions.

The Footprint asks a different question:

Did someone voluntarily choose to leave something behind?

Silence remains silence.

We should not turn it into inferred data.

What the Footprint Can Tell Us

If someone voluntarily selects a country and leaves a reflection, we can know exactly that:

A Footprint was submitted with that selected country and that reflection.

We should be careful not to quietly turn that into stronger claims such as:

All our learners come from these places.

or:

This represents everyone who completed the Guides.

It doesn't.

It represents the people who chose to participate in the Footprint.

That limitation should remain visible.

A Small Provenance Record

The Footprint itself can remain minimal while the system preserves enough non-identifying provenance to understand what kind of submission it is.

For example, a future implementation might distinguish:

Guide:
Awareness

Footprint:
Country      = optional
Reflection   = optional

Submission:
Voluntary    = yes

If a Guide or workflow version becomes important later, that can be considered during implementation.

But again:

Don't collect tomorrow's data today.

The AACCC Check

Before adding or changing the Student Footprint system, turn the Five Guides onto it.

Awareness

What information are we actually collecting?

Not what we think we're collecting.

What reaches the system?

Alignment

Does each piece of collected information correspond to a real need?

If not, remove it.

Coherence

Does the implementation actually match our privacy promise?

If the page says one thing while the system quietly does another, we have a coherence problem.

Clarity

Can an ordinary learner understand what happens when they submit a Footprint?

Privacy language should not require a lawyer to decode it.

Continuity

If the implementation changes later, does the privacy promise still hold?

A future feature should not quietly invalidate the reason learners trusted the original design.

One Rule Above the Rest

The Student Footprint exists because we deliberately chose not to solve curiosity with surveillance.

We may wonder:

Who is visiting?

But the answer does not have to be:

Let's watch everyone.

Instead, we can say:

We won't try to identify you simply because you visited.

and:

If you'd like to let usnow that you were here, you can cose to leave a tiny Footprint.

That preserves the difference between:

being observed

and:

choosing to be seen.

Continue

The Student Footprint is optional.

Learning isn't.

Return to the Five Guides:

Awareness → Alignment → Coherence → Clarity → Continuity

And if you eventually decide to leave a small note on the corkboard...

thank you for saying hello. 🙂
